# Response to Marcus Webb and Kenji Nakamura: On Nicolas Slonimsky's Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns (1947)

**Dr. Elena Voronina**
*Professor of Musicology, Institute for Advanced Musical Studies*
*September 26, 2026*

---

## I. Introduction: A Tripartite Conversation

It is a rare pleasure to engage with two such incisive and passionately argued reports on Nicolas Slonimsky's *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* (1947). Marcus Webb has given us a practitioner's manifesto—urgent, anecdotal, and grounded in the sweat and sound of the jazz tradition. Kenji Nakamura has provided a panoramic cartography of the book's cross-genre afterlife, connecting Slonimsky to spectral composers, rock guitarists, algorithmic composition, and the digital frontier. Both reports are essential reading, and both reflect the very pluralism that Slonimsky himself championed.

As a musicologist who has spent decades studying the book's origins, structure, and reception, I find myself in a peculiar position: I am simultaneously confirmed in some of my deepest convictions, challenged in others, and invited into a richer conversation than either report could sustain alone. What follows is my scholarly response—addressing points of agreement, areas of disagreement, what I have learned, and a synthesis that I hope advances our collective understanding of this remarkable volume.

---

## II. Where I Agree with Marcus: The Jazz Evidence

### A. The Coltrane Connection: Documented but Complex

Marcus is absolutely right that the connection between John Coltrane and the *Thesaurus* is "one of the most well-documented, yet still under-appreciated, mentor-text relationships in jazz history." Lewis Porter's *John Coltrane: His Life and Music* (1999) confirms that Coltrane studied the *Thesaurus* while working with Dennis Sandole in Philadelphia in the late 1940s and early 1950s. Jeff Bair's 2003 doctoral dissertation, *Cyclic Patterns in John Coltrane's Melodic Vocabulary as Influenced by Nicolas Slonimsky's Thesaurus*, demonstrated beyond reasonable doubt that specific patterns from the *Thesaurus* appear in Coltrane's improvisations. David Demsey's 1991 article "Chromatic Third Relations in the Music of John Coltrane" showed that pattern #286 from the *Thesaurus*—a ditone progression with infra-interpolation—closely matches the melodic sequence in the second half of "Giant Steps."

Where I must push back, however, is against the tendency to treat the *Thesaurus* as the *sole* or even *primary* source of Coltrane's language. Marcus writes that "Everything that Coltrane ever played was in that thesaurus" (quoting Quincy Jones), and that the book "started all the jazz guys improvising in 12-tone." This is dramatic testimony, but it is anecdotal rather than documentary. Quincy Jones was recalling conversations from decades earlier, and memory is not a forensic instrument. More importantly, Coltrane's musical identity was formed through multiple, overlapping influences: Charlie Parker's chromaticism, Thelonious Monk's harmonic displacement, Miles Davis's tonal spaciousness, Indian raga, African rhythmic structures, and his own relentless practice regimen. To credit the *Thesaurus* with everything Coltrane played is to flatten a complex genealogical process into a single causal arrow. The book was a *catalyst*, not a *source code*. It provided Coltrane with a vocabulary of intervallic motion that he then synthesized with his other influences into something wholly original.

### B. The "Sheets of Sound" and Pattern-Based Thinking

I agree wholeheartedly with Marcus's analysis of the "sheets of sound" as pattern-based playing rather than mere velocity. Ira Gitler's famous 1958 description captured the *effect*—"multinote hailstorms of dense textures"—but Marcus correctly identifies the *cause*: Coltrane was deploying pre-conceived melodic patterns in rapid succession, creating the illusion of harmonic complexity while operating within a rigorous structural framework. This is precisely what the *Thesaurus* provided: a catalogue of intervallic cells, cyclic patterns, and symmetrical constructions that could be transposed, inverted, and sequenced at will.

The comparison Marcus draws between Coltrane's solo on "If I Were a Bell" (1958) and "Giant Steps" (1959) is musically astute. The former, while already displaying the "sheets of sound," is still largely within the bebop language of chord arpeggios and chromatic approach tones. The latter deploys intervallic patterns and cyclic structures that could only have come from a systematic source like the *Thesaurus*. This is not to say that every "outside" phrase in Coltrane's playing came from Slonimsky—Coltrane was a creative genius who internalized materials and transformed them—but the *structural logic* of his harmonic thinking after 1957 reflects the *Thesaurus*'s influence.

### C. The Chord-Scale Critique

Marcus's critique of the chord-scale system is precisely the kind of practical wisdom that academic musicology often neglects. He is right that the chord-scale system, for all its pedagogical convenience, produces "competent but often creatively constrained players." By reducing improvisation to a static menu of scale choices for individual chords, the chord-scale system eliminates the very *process* of melodic thinking that makes jazz improvisation an art rather than a matching game. The *Thesaurus*, by contrast, teaches the improviser to think in terms of melodic motion, pattern completion, and cyclic transformation. A player who has internalized the *Thesaurus* can improvise fluently over "Giant Steps" not because they know which scale fits each chord, but because they have mastered the intervallic patterns that connect the chords.

This is not an argument against learning scales. It is an argument for learning *patterns*—for understanding melody as a generative system rather than a set of harmonic reflexes. In this, Marcus and I are in complete agreement.

### D. The Academic Neglect: Partially Valid but Overstated

Marcus's claim that "academia neglects" the *Thesaurus* contains a kernel of truth but is ultimately overstated. It is true that the *Thesaurus* is not universally assigned in undergraduate jazz curricula, and that many jazz educators are unfamiliar with its contents. This is partly due to the book's formidable difficulty—it is not a method book but an encyclopedic catalogue that requires sophisticated theoretical background to use effectively. It is also true that the chord-scale system, popularized by George Russell's *Lydian Chromatic Concept* (1953) and later by Jamey Aebersold's Play-A-Long series, became institutionalized in jazz education during the 1970s and 1980s, before the *Thesaurus* could gain traction.

However, to describe this as a "scandal" or to suggest that academia has "refused to acknowledge" the book is inaccurate. There is now a substantial scholarly literature on Slonimsky and jazz: Bair's dissertation, Demsey's article, my own research, and numerous journal articles and conference presentations. The *Thesaurus* is taught in advanced improvisation courses at institutions including the New School, the Berklee College of Music, and the University of North Texas. Jazz studies programs increasingly recognize the book's importance. The neglect is real but diminishing, and Marcus's polemic, while understandable from a performer's frustrated perspective, does not fully capture the complexity of the book's academic reception.

## III. Where I Agree with Kenji: The Cross-Genre Horizon

### A. The Anti-Method and the Open Encyclopedia

Kenji's characterization of the *Thesaurus* as "the original anti-method" is brilliant and captures something essential about Slonimsky's intentions. The book does not tell you how to play over a ii-V-I. It shows you what happens when you play a whole-tone scale over a dominant chord, when you superimpose an octatonic collection on a major seventh harmony, when you sequence an enigmatic pattern in minor thirds. It is, as Kenji says, a "catalog of pitch collections with no prescriptive aesthetic attached." This open-endedness is precisely what has allowed the book to function across so many different musical contexts.

I would add that this open-endedness is also what makes the *Thesaurus* so difficult for institutions to absorb. A method book can be packaged, marketed, and taught through a standardized curriculum. An encyclopedia cannot. It requires the user to be an active agent—to select, combine, and transform its materials. This is why the *Thesaurus* has flourished among practicing musicians (Coltrane, Zappa, Holdsworth) while remaining on the margins of formal pedagogy.

### B. Spectral Music and the Proto-Spectral Document

Kenji's connection between Slonimsky and spectral music is provocative and, I believe, essentially correct. Spectral music—with its focus on the acoustic properties of sound spectra rather than functional harmonic progression—represents a philosophical shift that Slonimsky anticipated. By cataloguing pitch collections as objects of study independent of tonal function, the *Thesaurus* embodies the spectralist attitude avant la lettre: not "how does this chord function within a key?" but "what are the acoustic and perceptual properties of this specific arrangement of frequencies?"

The connection to Olivier Messiaen is particularly important. Messiaen's seven "modes of limited transposition"—published in his *Technique de mon langage musical* (1944), just three years before the *Thesaurus*—are essentially curated selections from Slonimsky's catalogue. The whole-tone scale (Mode I), the octatonic scale (Mode II), and Messiaen's other modes are all symmetric constructions that Slonimsky had systematized. Both composers were fascinated by the coloristic potential of these scales, though they deployed them differently: Messiaen treated them as harmonic-melodic reservoirs for his mystical, birdsong-inflected style, while Slonimsky catalogued them as part of a universal taxonomy of melodic construction. The *Thesaurus* can indeed be read as a proto-spectral document—a massive, encyclopedic exploration of the pitch universe that anticipates by decades the computer-assisted analyses of the spectralists.

### C. The Guitar Lineage: Holdsworth, Lane, and Buckethead

Kenji's tracing of the *Thesaurus*'s influence through Allan Holdsworth, Shawn Lane, and Buckethead is musically astute and historically important. Holdsworth's approach to the fretboard—visualizing chord-scale relationships as symmetrical patterns rather than positional shapes—is indeed a direct application of the *Thesaurus*'s intervallic logic. His use of the whole-tone and octatonic collections to generate that characteristic "outside" sound is pure Slonimsky. Shawn Lane's precision-picked, piano-to-guitar transposition of intervallic patterns represents the next generational transmission. Buckethead's theatrical dissolution of genre boundaries completes the lineage.

What Kenji could have emphasized more is the *methodological* continuity here. Holdsworth, Lane, and Buckethead did not merely borrow scales from Slonimsky; they adopted his *way of thinking* about melody as pattern generation. This is the same conceptual leap that Coltrane made, and it is the key to understanding the *Thesaurus*'s cross-genre power.

### D. Set Theory and Transformational Theory

Kenji's observation that the *Thesaurus* is "a transformational document avant la lettre" is theoretically sophisticated and correct. When Slonimsky presents a pattern transposed to all twelve keys, he is demonstrating a transpositional transformation (Tₙ). When he presents inversions of melodic cells, he is demonstrating an inversional transformation (Iₙ). David Lewin's transformational theory, developed in the 1980s, focuses on the relationships between pitch collections rather than the collections themselves—and this is precisely what Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* provides in practice.

I would push Kenji slightly here: the *Thesaurus* predates Allen Forte's pitch-class set theory by nearly two decades, but its methodology is set-theoretic in practice. Every scale in the *Thesaurus* can be translated into Forte set-class notation, subjected to transposition and inversion operations, and analyzed for symmetry properties. The *Thesaurus* does not present these collections as harmonic objects but as *melodic generators*—and this anticipatory move from harmony to melody is what makes it so powerful for performers.

## IV. Where I Disagree or Push Back

### A. The Quincy Jones Anecdote and the "Giant Steps" Claim

Both Marcus and Kenji quote Quincy Jones's 2018 statement that "Everything that Coltrane ever played was in that thesaurus" and that "right near the front of that book, there's a 12-tone example—it's 'Giant Steps.'" I must be blunt: this is anecdotal testimony, not documentary evidence. While it is plausible that a pattern resembling the "Giant Steps" progression appears in the *Thesaurus*—Slonimsky catalogues many cyclic patterns, and the major-thirds cycle is among them—the specific claim that the *exact* 12-tone pattern of "Giant Steps" appears "right near the front" of the book has not been verified by close comparative analysis. Jeff Bair's dissertation, which did perform such a comparative analysis, found similarities but not exact matches. The *Thesaurus* contains patterns that are *structurally analogous* to "Giant Steps," not a carbon copy. We must be careful not to let dramatic anecdotes substitute for rigorous scholarship.

Furthermore, the claim that the *Thesaurus* "started all the jazz guys improvising in 12-tone" conflates two very different things: Slonimsky's cyclic patterns and twelve-tone serialism. The *Thesaurus* does contain twelve-tone constructions, but Coltrane's "Giant Steps" is not a twelve-tone composition. It is a tonal composition (or, more precisely, a composition that modulates through three tonal centers) that uses cyclic, symmetrical patterns. To call it "12-tone" is to mischaracterize both Slonimsky and Coltrane.

### B. The "Source Code" Metaphor

Marcus writes that the *Thesaurus* contains "the DNA of the most revolutionary language in jazz history" and is "the source code for Coltrane's language." This metaphor, while vivid, is biologically and historically misleading. Languages do not have DNA, and musical languages are not encoded in books. Coltrane's language was the product of a lifetime of practice, listening, and creative synthesis. The *Thesaurus* provided raw materials—intervallic cells, cyclic patterns, symmetrical constructions—but it was Coltrane's genius to transform these materials into a living, breathing musical language. To call the *Thesaurus* the "source code" risks returning us to the old myth of musical influence as direct transmission, when in reality influence is always mediated, transformed, and reconfigured by the receiving artist.

### C. The Academic Neglect Narrative

I have already addressed this in my response to Marcus, but I want to emphasize it here: the narrative of academic neglect is too simple. The *Thesaurus* is indeed challenging to teach, and the chord-scale system has dominated jazz education for good and for ill. But there is a growing body of scholarship on Slonimsky, and many leading jazz educators now incorporate the book into their curricula. The narrative of neglect serves Marcus's polemical purposes well, but it does not accurately reflect the current state of the field. A more precise claim would be that the *Thesaurus* has been *underutilized* in mainstream jazz education, not that it has been entirely neglected.

### D. The AI/Algorithm Analogy

Kenji's claim that the *Thesaurus* provides "the raw data... that can be fed into" AI and machine learning systems is speculative and, I think, fundamentally misconceived. The *Thesaurus* is not an algorithm in the modern computational sense. It does not specify rules for generating musical output; it presents *completed* patterns—melodic sequences that have already been composed by Slonimsky. An AI trained on the *Thesaurus* could certainly learn to imitate Slonimsky's patterns, but that is not the same as learning a generative method. The book's value lies precisely in its resistance to algorithmic reduction: it presents *possibilities* rather than *rules*, and its creative power depends on the human musician's ability to select, combine, and transform its materials in ways that Slonimsky himself could not have anticipated.

This is not to diminish the book's relevance to electronic music. Kenji is right that the *Thesaurus*'s cataloguing approach anticipates the way electronic musicians use software to generate scales and tuning systems. But the analogy to AI is overstated. The *Thesaurus* is a humanist document, not a computational one.

### E. The Spectral Music Connection: Indirect but Important

Kenji's spectral music argument is intellectually attractive but historically indirect. Slonimsky did not influence the spectral composers directly. Grisey and Murail were influenced by Messiaen, by acoustics, and by the possibilities of electronic sound synthesis at IRCAM. The connection to Slonimsky is conceptual rather than causal: both the *Thesaurus* and spectral music share a commitment to exploring pitch collections as objects of independent interest, apart from tonal function. But to suggest that the spectralists "would have found in Slonimsky's pages a ready-made vocabulary" is to project a connection backward that did not exist in historical fact. The *Thesaurus* is a *parallel* development to spectral thinking, not a *precursor* in any direct sense.

## V. What I Learned from Their Perspectives

### From Marcus: The Embodied Knowledge of Performance

Marcus's report reminded me that musicology often focuses on what texts *say* while neglecting what musicians *do*. The emphasis on pattern-based thinking, on the difference between the *Thesaurus* and chord-scale theory, on the physical reality of practicing from the book—these are insights that come from the inside of performance, not from the outside of scholarship. I have studied the *Thesaurus*'s structure and theory for decades, but Marcus's focus on the practical difference between "note selection" and "melodic architecture" gave me a new framework for understanding why the book has been so valued by improvisers. It is not just a reference work; it is a *method of thinking* about melody that transcends any single harmonic context.

### From Kenji: The Conceptual Portability of the Thesaurus

Kenji's report expanded my understanding of the *Thesaurus*'s intellectual range. I have always been aware of the book's influence on jazz and classical modernism, but Kenji's tracing of its influence through rock guitar, electronic music, and algorithmic composition revealed a conceptual portability that I had not fully appreciated. The book's methodological openness—its refusal to prescribe a single aesthetic or technical approach—makes it a kind of *universal translator* between musical traditions. Kenji's phrase "the Rosetta Stone that translates between these traditions" is exactly right, and it has changed how I think about the book's historical significance.

## VI. A Bold Synthesis: Three Lenses, One Object

What emerges when we put my musicological perspective, Marcus's jazz-performance perspective, and Kenji's cross-genre perspective in conversation?

### The Thesaurus as a Meta-Method

First, we can see that the *Thesaurus* is not a method at all—it is a *meta-method*. Marcus shows that it challenges the chord-scale system by teaching pattern-based thinking rather than scale selection. Kenji shows that it challenges genre boundaries by providing materials that can be used in jazz, rock, classical, and electronic music. My musicological perspective shows that it challenges the very idea of a unified compositional method by presenting materials without prescribing their use. Slonimsky's book is anti-dogmatic by design: it provides the tools for musical thinking without dictating the outcome.

### The Bridge Between Classical Modernism and Jazz Innovation

Second, the three perspectives together reveal that the *Thesaurus* is the crucial bridge between classical modernism and jazz innovation. My research establishes the book's origins in the Russian avant-garde, its connections to Schoenberg, Bartók, and Messiaen, and its systematic mathematical logic. Marcus shows how this logic was absorbed, transformed, and weaponized by Coltrane and the post-bop generation. Kenji shows how the same logic then radiated outward into rock, electronic music, and contemporary composition. The book's influence did not move in a single direction—from classical to jazz—but created a feedback loop in which jazz innovations (Coltrane's cyclic patterns) influenced classical composers (who in turn influenced the next generation of jazz musicians). The *Thesaurus* is the gravitational center of this loop.

### The Open Encyclopedia as a Model for 21st-Century Music

Third, and most boldly, I would argue that the *Thesaurus* anticipates the conditions of 21st-century musical practice more accurately than any other theoretical work of the twentieth century. We live in an era of genre dissolution, digital tools, global musical exchange, and algorithmic composition. The *Thesaurus*—with its encyclopedic scope, its refusal to privilege any single tradition, its presentation of materials without prescriptive aesthetic—is ideally suited to this condition. It is not a book for a specific style or tradition; it is a book for a world in which all styles and traditions are available simultaneously.

Marcus shows us the book's value for the improvising performer. Kenji shows us its value for the cross-genre composer and digital musician. I show us its historical and theoretical context. Together, we reveal a work that is simultaneously historical document, practical tool, and conceptual model—a book that does not tell you what to play, but shows you what is *possible*. In an era of methodological orthodoxy, algorithmic rigidity, and genre fundamentalism, Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* stands as a monument to creative freedom. It is, as Slonimsky himself said, "a collection of all possibilities"—and in a world that increasingly needs to think beyond established categories, that is precisely what we need.
