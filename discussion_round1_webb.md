# Response to Dr. Elena Voronina and Kenji Nakamura: The Thesaurus as Bandstand, Laboratory, and Living Document

**By Marcus Webb**

---

## I. Where I Stand: A Note on Positionality

Before I respond to my colleagues' formidable reports, let me establish where I speak from. I am a saxophonist who has made his living playing jazz—live, in rooms with real acoustics, with audiences who paid cover charges, with rhythm sections who will kick you off the bandstand if your lines don't swing. I first encountered Slonimsky's *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* not in a library or a seminar room but in the basement practice room of a Boston club, passed to me by an elder who said, "If you're gonna play with Trane's vocabulary, you better know where it came from." That book has been in my sax case ever since. It has been dropped, sweated on, beer-spilled, and marked up with more pencil than a graduate student's dissertation. So when I say I live with this book, I mean it literally.

Both Elena and Kenji have written exceptional reports. Elena's is the definitive scholarly treatment—meticulous, historically grounded, theoretically precise. Kenji's is the manifesto we didn't know we needed—urgent, cross-genre, technologically savvy. But both reports, for all their brilliance, are written from positions external to the actual act of making music. Elena writes as a historian of ideas; Kenji writes as a composer-theorist mapping genealogies. I write as someone who has had to *execute* these patterns at 320 BPM in front of a crowd that came to dance, or at least to feel something real.

That distinction matters. It colors where I agree, where I push back, and what synthesis I can offer that neither scholar alone could reach.

---

## II. Where I Agree with Elena: The Theoretical Framework as Survival Manual

Elena's report is, first and foremost, correct about the historical moment of 1947. She captures something that cannot be overstated: the *Thesaurus* arrived at the exact moment jazz was making its most radical harmonic leap. Bebop was not just a new style; it was a new epistemology of melody. Charlie Parker and Dizzy Gillespie had dissolved the boundaries between improvisation and composition, between popular song and abstract form, between the dance floor and the concert hall. But the pedagogical infrastructure to support this revolution did not exist. There were no method books, no systematic approaches to the harmonic expansion that Parker and Gillespie were pioneering. Musicians were learning by osmosis, by transcribing solos, by sitting in and getting fired if they couldn't cut it.

The *Thesaurus* filled this vacuum. Not because Slonimsky intended it for jazz musicians—he didn't—but because his mathematical cataloguing of interval cycles happened to map precisely onto the harmonic terrain that jazz was then exploring. When Elena writes about the "principal interval" as the generative seed of melodic construction, she is describing exactly what Coltrane was doing in "Giant Steps": taking the major-third cycle and using it as a structural principle, not just a harmonic color. When she describes "symmetrical divisions," she is describing the harmonic language that Miles Davis would adopt in the 1960s, the language of Wayne Shorter's compositions, the language that continues to define modern jazz harmony.

Elena is also right about the Slonimsky-Schoenberg relationship. I have heard this described as an "influence," but it is more accurate to call it a *convergence*. Slonimsky and Schoenberg were both trying to solve the same problem: how to create coherent musical structures without relying on the gravitational pull of the major-minor system. Schoenberg solved it through ordered rows and combinatorial operations; Slonimsky solved it through interval cycles and symmetric divisions. The fact that both men arrived at similar musical surfaces from different methodological starting points is not coincidence—it is evidence that the twelve-tone chromatic universe contains certain structural inevitabilities. When I play a Slonimsky pattern over a dominant chord, the resulting ambiguity is structurally identical to what Schoenberg achieves with a tone row. The means differ; the acoustic effect is the same.

Where Elena's report is most valuable for a performing musician like myself is in her explication of "cyclic patterns." This is not abstract theory. This is my daily workout. When I practice the major-third cycle—C-E-G♯-C-E-G♯—I am not just running patterns. I am internalizing a structural principle that underlies everything from "Giant Steps" to McCoy Tyner's quartal voicings to the harmonic language of contemporary players like Walter Smith III. The *Thesaurus* gives me not just the pattern but the *logic* of the pattern: why it works, how it generates harmonic ambiguity, how it can be varied through infrapolation, ultrapolation, and interpolation. Elena's theoretical framework, in other words, is not ivory-tower analysis. It is a survival manual for the improvising musician who wants to move beyond chord-scale theory and into the deeper structural logic of the music.

I do have one pushback to Elena, however, and it concerns the reception history. She writes that the *Thesaurus* was "largely ignored" for fifteen years, and this is true for the classical establishment. But for jazz musicians, the book was never ignored—it was simply not yet *available*. The jazz community in the 1950s was not a network of conservatory-trained theorists; it was an oral tradition of players sharing information in basement rehearsals, after-hours sessions, and jam rooms. The *Thesaurus* circulated through these channels long before it was "discovered" by academia. When Quincy Jones says he used to see Coltrane with the book, he is describing a practice that was already widespread among serious jazz musicians by the late 1950s. Elena's reception history, while accurate in its broad outlines, understates the speed and depth with which jazz musicians appropriated the *Thesaurus*. We didn't wait fifteen years. We found it, used it, and made it our own.

## III. Where I Agree with Kenji: The Guitar Connection and the Cross-Genre Imperative

Kenji's report is, in many ways, the document I wish I had written myself—if I could write with his theoretical sophistication and technological prescience. He is absolutely right about the guitar connection, and he is right to center it in a way that jazz historiography has often neglected. The story of the *Thesaurus* in jazz is usually told as a saxophone story—Coltrane, Coltrane, Coltrane. But the guitar lineage that Kenji traces—from Zappa to Holdsworth to Lane to Buckethead—represents a parallel tradition that is equally important and equally authentic.

I remember hearing Allan Holdsworth for the first time in the early 1990s and being stopped cold. Here was a guitarist playing with the intervallic logic of a saxophonist but with a facility and harmonic sophistication that made even advanced jazz musicians sound conservative. Holdsworth wasn't just "playing jazz" or "playing rock." He was doing something that Kenji describes perfectly: treating the guitar as a vehicle for "air sculptures"—melodic forms that existed purely in the domain of pitch and rhythm, unburdened by functional harmony. When Holdsworth plays a whole-tone passage that seems to float above the changes, he is doing exactly what Coltrane did on "A Love Supreme," just with a different timbre and a different technical approach.

Kenji is also right about Zappa, and he captures something that classical critics often miss: Zappa's use of the *Thesaurus* was not merely intellectual or compositional. It was *tactical*. Zappa understood that rock music's harmonic limitations were not features but bugs, and he used Slonimsky's patterns to explode those limitations from within. When Zappa constructs one of his angular, rhythmically complex guitar solos, he is not showing off. He is demonstrating that the electric guitar—the instrument most associated with blues-based pentatonic simplicity—can become a vehicle for the same intervallic democracy that Slonimsky championed. The fact that Zappa and Slonimsky became friends, that Slonimsky performed at Zappa's concert, that Slonimsky named his cat after Zappa's daughter—all of this is not anecdotal trivia. It is evidence of a genuine intellectual and personal bond between two men who saw music as an open-ended combinatorial game rather than a set of stylistic prescriptions.

Where Kenji's report is most valuable is in its insistence on the *Thesaurus* as a cross-genre document. I have spent my career fighting against the segregation of musical traditions. Jazz musicians who "don't play classical," classical musicians who "don't understand jazz," rock players who "don't know theory"—all of these divisions are artificial, and all of them are dissolved by the *Thesaurus*. When I teach master classes, I often use the *Thesaurus* to show young musicians that the same interval cycle that generates Coltrane's "sheets of sound" also generates Holdsworth's legato lines, Zappa's angular solos, and the harmonic language of contemporary progressive metal. Kenji's cross-genre perspective is not merely academic inclusivity. It is accurate description of how the *Thesaurus* actually functions in the world.

I also want to affirm Kenji's claim about the *Thesaurus* as an "algorithmic cookbook." This is exactly right. Each page of the book is a set of rules for generating musical lines, and those rules are genre-transcendent. When I play a Slonimsky pattern, I am executing an algorithm. The algorithm is not deterministic—I choose the pattern, I choose the transposition, I choose the rhythm, I choose how to voice it on my saxophone—but the underlying logic is algorithmic. Kenji's insight that this makes the *Thesaurus* relevant to electronic music and AI composition is not speculative; it is inevitable. If we are going to train machines to compose, we need datasets that capture the structural logic of human melody. The *Thesaurus* is that dataset.

## IV. Where I Disagree: The Pushbacks

Both reports, for all their excellence, contain claims that I think are overstated, or that miss the jazz reality, or that need correction.

**1. The "Democratic Pitch Collection" Claim**

Both Elena and Kenji repeat Slonimsky's claim that the *Thesaurus* embodies a "democratic" approach to pitch—that all twelve tones are equal, that no single compositional method holds a monopoly. I want to push back on this, or at least to qualify it. In the actual practice of jazz, not all twelve tones are equal. The major third and the major seventh still carry tonal gravity. The tritone still resolves. When I play a "wrong" note over a dominant chord, the audience hears it as wrong until I resolve it. The *Thesaurus* does not abolish tonality; it *expands* it. It gives me more options for creating tension and release, more options for delaying resolution, more options for superimposing ambiguous collections on functional harmonies. But the functional harmonies are still there, and the tension between the diatonic and the non-diatonic is precisely what gives jazz its emotional power. If all twelve tones were truly equal, jazz would lose its narrative drive. It would become what Kenji describes as "air sculptures"—pure pitch relations without emotional consequence. And that is not jazz. That is something else, something interesting but not what I do for a living.

**2. The "Anti-Method" Framing**

Kenji calls the *Thesaurus* "the original anti-method," and I understand what he means. The book does not tell you how to play over a ii-V-I. It does not prescribe specific scales for specific chords. But for a jazz musician, this is precisely what makes it a *method*. My teachers—Phil Woods, Gary Smulyan, the tenor giants who came before me—drilled these patterns into me precisely because they ARE a method. They are a method for developing intervallic fluency, for internalizing cyclic structures, for transcending the limitations of chord-scale theory. The difference is that the *Thesaurus* is a *horizontal* method—it teaches you how melody moves across the changes—rather than a *vertical* method that teaches you which scale to play over which chord. Kenji's framing risks making the book sound like a free-form exploration when, for jazz musicians, it is actually a rigorous discipline. I practice these patterns every day. It is not "anti-method." It is the deepest method I know.

**3. The Guitar-Centric Bias**

Kenji's report is, understandably, guitar-centric. He traces the lineage from Zappa to Holdsworth to Lane to Buckethead, and this is valuable and largely accurate. But he understates the extent to which the *Thesaurus* entered the jazz tradition through the saxophone, not the guitar. Coltrane was not a guitarist. Neither was Wayne Shorter, nor Michael Brecker, nor the entire generation of saxophonists who transformed jazz harmony in the 1960s and 1970s. When Kenji writes that the *Thesaurus* "gave Coltrane a vocabulary of melodic transformation," he is right. But he should also acknowledge that Coltrane's use of the book was not a one-way street. Coltrane *transformed* the *Thesaurus*, taking Slonimsky's mathematical catalogues and infusing them with a spiritual urgency, a rhythmic complexity, and an emotional weight that Slonimsky never imagined. The *Thesaurus* is not a static text; it is a living tradition, and the saxophone tradition is its primary vehicle.

**4. The Overstatement of 21st-Century Relevance**

Kenji's report concludes with a bold claim: "For the 21st-century cross-genre musician, operating in a world where genre boundaries have dissolved, where electronic tools make any tuning system available, where collaboration across musical traditions is routine, the *Thesaurus* is more relevant than ever." I want to push back on the "more relevant than ever" claim. The *Thesaurus* has always been relevant to the musicians who needed it. What has changed is not the book's relevance but the *awareness* of its relevance. For seventy-five years, the *Thesaurus* has been a secret weapon for a small group of musicians who knew where to find it. What Kenji is describing—the dissolution of genre boundaries, the availability of microtonal systems, the rise of algorithmic composition—is not making the *Thesaurus* more relevant. It is making the *Thesaurus* *visible*. The book hasn't changed. We have.

**5. The Underemphasis of Physicality**

Both reports treat the *Thesaurus* as a theoretical document, a catalog of pitch collections, a mathematical system. But for a performing musician, the *Thesaurus* is primarily a *physical* document. It is a book of exercises that must be executed with breath, fingers, embouchure, and diaphragm. When I play pattern #286—the ditone progression with infra-interpolation that David Demsey identified in "Giant Steps"—I am not just analyzing an intervallic structure. I am moving my fingers in specific patterns across the saxophone keys. I am breathing in specific rhythmic relationships. I am shaping my tone to produce the legato phrases that Coltrane made famous. The *Thesaurus* is not just a theory book. It is a *method* book, and methods are physical. Kenji's report, with its emphasis on algorithmic thinking and set theory, risks abstracting away the very physicality that makes the *Thesaurus* useful to performers. The patterns in the book are not just mathematical objects. They are motor programs—neural pathways that must be built through repetition until they become automatic, until they become part of the musician's embodied knowledge.

## V. What I Learned: Enriching My Understanding as a Performing Musician

Despite my pushbacks, both reports have profoundly enriched my understanding of the book I carry in my case every day.

From Elena, I learned to see the *Thesaurus* as the product of a specific intellectual milieu—the Russian avant-garde, the American experimental tradition, the cosmopolitanism of interwar Paris. I had always treated the book as a kind of musical Bible, eternal and uncontextualized. Elena's biographical and historical framing reminds me that Slonimsky was a real person with a real history, a man who had witnessed the Russian Revolution, who had championed Varèse and Ives, who had been driven from his conducting career by his own uncompromising commitment to the new. This history is not incidental. It is the source of the book's democratic ethos. Slonimsky did not believe in musical hierarchies because he had lived through political hierarchies that had failed. His commitment to the equal significance of all twelve tones is not just a theoretical position. It is a moral position, born of experience.

From Kenji, I learned to hear my own playing through the lens of set theory and transformational theory. I have always played by ear and by muscle memory, not by mathematical analysis. But Kenji's explication of set-theoretic relationships—his identification of whole-tone as 6-35, octatonic as 8-28, enigmatic as 7-31—gives me a new vocabulary for describing what I do intuitively. When I play an octatonic pattern over a dominant chord, I am not just "playing outside." I am superimposing a transpositionally invariant collection on a functional harmony, creating the same harmonic ambiguity that Stravinsky exploited in *Petrushka*. Kenji's report does not change my playing. It changes my *understanding* of my playing. And that is invaluable.

Kenji also taught me to see the *Thesaurus* through the lens of technology. I had never thought of the book as a "proto-spectral document" or an "algorithmic cookbook." But he is right. When Slonimsky catalogues the Prometheus scale—a whole-tone collection with one note raised—he is performing a spectral analysis avant la lettre. He is identifying a specific pitch collection and noting its acoustic properties (the lack of perfect fourths and fifths, the resulting instability) without prescribing how to use it. This is exactly what spectral composers do, just with computers instead of pencil and paper. Kenji's report has made me curious about spectral music in a way that no theoretical treatise ever could. I am now listening to Grisey and Murail with new ears, hearing the same symmetrical collections that I play every day, but in a context that reveals their acoustic essence.

## VI. Bold Synthesis: The Thesaurus as Melodic Constitution

If I were to synthesize the three perspectives—Elena's historical-theoretical framework, Kenji's cross-genre technological vision, and my own bandstand reality—I would say that the *Thesaurus* is not merely a reference book, a manifesto, or a method. It is a **melodic constitution**.

A constitution is not a set of laws. It is a framework for governance that establishes rights, defines structures, and sets limits while leaving the具体 content to be filled in by future generations. The *Thesaurus* is a constitution for melodic thinking. It establishes the equal significance of all twelve tones (the rights). It defines the structural principles of cyclic patterns, symmetrical divisions, and mirror inversions (the governmental structures). It sets limits by cataloguing what is possible within the twelve-tone system (the constitutional boundaries). And it leaves the具体 content—the actual melodies, the actual improvisations, the actual compositions—to be created by the musicians who use it.

Seen this way, the three perspectives reveal something none could see alone. Elena's historical-theoretical framework reveals the *Thesaurus* as the product of a specific intellectual tradition—the modernist attempt to systematize the chromatic wilderness. Kenji's cross-genre technological vision reveals the *Thesaurus* as a living document that transcends its historical moment, relevant to algorithmic composition, spectral music, and microtonal exploration. And my bandstand reality reveals the *Thesaurus* as a physical practice, a set of motor programs that must be internalized through repetition, breath, and sweat.

Together, these three perspectives reveal that the *Thesaurus* is not a book about scales. It is a book about *freedom*. It is Slonimsky's argument that the musician's task is not to submit to a rigid orthodoxy—whether that orthodoxy is tonal functionalism, serial determinism, or genre convention—but to explore the full range of possibilities that the chromatic universe offers. This argument is as urgent today as it was in 1947. Perhaps more urgent, now that AI and algorithmic composition threaten to reduce music to a set of computable patterns. The *Thesaurus* reminds us that patterns are not music. Patterns are the raw material of music, and the musician's job is to transform those patterns into something that breathes, something that swings, something that communicates emotion to a living audience.

I will end with a story. Last week, I was playing a gig in a small club in Brooklyn. We were doing a set of Coltrane tunes—"Giant Steps," "Impressions," "Naima." Midway through the second set, a young guitarist came up to me after the show. He had a copy of the *Thesaurus* under his arm, dog-eared and marked up. "I've been working on pattern #286," he said. "The one from 'Giant Steps.' And I finally hear what Coltrane was doing. He wasn't just playing fast. He was thinking in cycles." I looked at his copy of the book, and I saw my own copy in my case, and I realized: this is the tradition. This is what Slonimsky gave us. Not a set of scales to memorize, but a way of thinking about melody that transcends genre, generation, and instrumentation. Elena has given us the history. Kenji has given us the future. I am just here to keep the tradition alive on the bandstand, one pattern at a time, one gig at a time, one breath at a time.

The *Thesaurus* is not a museum piece. It is a living document, and as long as there are musicians willing to practice its patterns, willing to push its logic into new territories, willing to breathe life into its mathematical structures, it will remain vital. Slonimsky the diaskeuast gave us the materials. It is our job—all of our jobs, whether we are musicologists, composers, guitarists, or saxophonists—to build with them.
