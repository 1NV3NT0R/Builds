# Nicolas Slonimsky's "Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns" (1947): A Jazz Perspective

## By Marcus Webb, Grammy-nominated jazz saxophonist, composer, and music educator

---

### Introduction: The Book That Rewired Jazz

In 1947, a Russian-American musicologist named Nicolas Slonimsky published a strange, formidable volume entitled *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns*. It was not a jazz book. Slonimsky—conductor, lexicographer, diabolical logician of pitch—intended it as a comprehensive catalog of scalar and melodic materials for composers and performers working in the broader Western tradition. Yet within two decades, this same book would become the secret weapon behind the most revolutionary language in jazz history. It would be carried around by John Coltrane until its pages literally fell off. It would inspire harmonic innovations that redefined what it meant to improvise. And it would remain, for fifty years, the most important book in jazz that academia largely refused to acknowledge.

From a jazz perspective, the *Thesaurus* is not merely a reference work. It is a map of harmonic possibility that preceded and in many ways predicted the post-bop revolution. Where standard chord-scale theory offers a static menu of scale choices for individual chords, Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* presents the raw material of intervallic motion—patterns, cycles, and symmetrical constructions—that transcend any single harmony. This is precisely why it changed everything.

---

### 1. Coltrane's Relationship with the Thesaurus: Evidence and Impact

The connection between John Coltrane and Nicolas Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* is one of the most well-documented, yet still under-appreciated, mentor-text relationships in jazz history. The evidence is not merely anecdotal; it is corroborated by contemporaries, biographers, and the very DNA of Coltrane's compositions.

Quincy Jones, who worked extensively with Coltrane and observed him closely, stated in a 2018 interview: "Every time I used to see Coltrane, he'd have Nicolas Slonimsky's book." Jones went even further, declaring: "Everything that Coltrane ever played was in that thesaurus. In fact, right near the front of that book, there's a 12-tone example — it's 'Giant Steps.' Everyone thinks Coltrane wrote that, he didn't. It's Slonimsky. That book started all the jazz guys improvising in 12-tone. Coltrane carried that book around till the pages fell off."

This testimony from Quincy Jones is monumental. It suggests that Coltrane did not merely borrow isolated patterns from Slonimsky; he absorbed the book's entire intervallic logic and allowed it to reshape his conception of improvisation itself. Biographer Lewis Porter, in *John Coltrane: His Life and Music* (University of Michigan Press, 1999), confirms that Coltrane "explored contemporary techniques and theory" and "studied the *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* by Nicolas Slonimsky (1947)" while working with teacher Dennis Sandole at the Granoff School of Music in Philadelphia.

Coltrane's relationship with the book was not passive. He internalized its patterns to such an extent that they became indistinguishable from his own voice. The *Thesaurus* provided him with a vocabulary of cyclic patterns, symmetrical intervals, and chromatic voice-leading techniques that allowed him to escape the horizontal constraints of bebop. Where Charlie Parker had revolutionized jazz through chromatic approach tones and chord arpeggios, Coltrane would revolutionize it through vertical intervallic stacks and cyclical harmonic motion—precisely the territory Slonimsky had mapped.

### 2. Specific Cyclic Patterns in Coltrane's Work

The influence of Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* is most directly audible in Coltrane's compositions from the 1959-1960 period, particularly on the album *Giant Steps* (1960). Three tracks in particular demonstrate the application of Slonimsky-esque cyclic patterns: "Giant Steps," "Countdown," and "Cousin Mary."

**"Giant Steps"** is the most famous example. The composition's chord progression cycles through key centers a major third apart: B major, G major, E-flat major, and back. This creates what is now known as the "Coltrane changes"—a harmonic matrix built on major third relationships. The Wikipedia entry on Coltrane changes explicitly notes that Coltrane studied the *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* and that the augmented tonal cycles in "Giant Steps" reflect this study. The progression's opening bars descend in major thirds (B to G to E-flat), while the second section uses ascending ii-V-I progressions separated by major thirds. This is not merely a harmonic curiosity; it is a direct application of the intervallic cycling logic found throughout Slonimsky's book.

**"Countdown"** is equally revealing. The piece is a reharmonization of Eddie Vinson's "Tune Up," but where the original uses a straightforward ii-V-I progression, Coltrane's version substitutes rapid chromatic transitions. In the Wikipedia analysis of Coltrane changes, "Countdown" is described as using "a progression of chords that cycle back to the V-I at the end," with each chord receiving two beats in 4/4 time. The harmonic substitutions occur so rapidly that they "trick the listener into thinking that they are listening to a completely unrelated tune." This is classic Slonimsky: taking a familiar harmonic vehicle and overlaying a pattern of chromatic third-relations that creates the illusion of tonal dislocation while maintaining rigorous logical structure.

**"Cousin Mary"** provides a different but equally Slonimsky-inflected approach. While the piece is rooted in a more conventional blues framework, Coltrane's improvisation on the track exemplifies the "sheets of sound" technique—rapid, dense arpeggios and scale patterns that cascade across the chord changes. The *Thesaurus* provides exactly the kind of pre-composed melodic patterns that would allow a player to generate such torrential lines. Coltrane did not need to invent these patterns in the moment; he had already cataloged them in his mind from Slonimsky's pages.

### 3. How It Changed Jazz Improvisation Vocabulary in the Late 1950s-1960s

Before the *Thesaurus* filtered into the jazz mainstream through players like Coltrane, jazz improvisation vocabulary was largely governed by two traditions: the bebop language of chord arpeggios and chromatic approach tones (Parker, Gillespie), and the modal language of scale-based improvisation (Davis, Russell). The chord-scale system, which would later dominate jazz education, was still in its infancy.

Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* offered something entirely different: a catalogue of melodic patterns organized not by chord function but by intervallic structure. This allowed musicians to think in terms of vertical pitch collections and cyclic motion rather than horizontal chord progressions. The result was a radical expansion of the jazz improviser's vocabulary.

In the late 1950s and 1960s, this manifested in several ways:

- **Cyclic improvisation**: Players began to superimpose symmetrical patterns over chord changes, creating the effect of "playing outside" the harmony while maintaining an underlying structural logic.
- **Chromatic third-relations**: The use of major third cycles as a generative principle for both composition and improvisation became a hallmark of the post-bop language.
- **Dense linear patterns**: The "sheets of sound" approach—cascading arpeggios and scale fragments played at breakneck speed—was directly fueled by the pattern-based thinking Slonimsky's book encouraged.
- **Polytonal superimposition**: By providing patterns that could be transposed to any pitch level, the *Thesaurus* encouraged musicians to superimpose different tonal centers simultaneously, foreshadowing the free jazz movements of the 1960s.

The vocabulary shift was not merely theoretical; it was audible. Compare Coltrane's solo on "If I Were a Bell" from the 1958 Miles Davis sextet recordings with his solo on "Giant Steps" from 1959. The former, while already displaying the "sheets of sound" approach, is still largely within the bebop language. The latter, by contrast, deploys intervallic patterns and cyclic structures that could only have come from a source like Slonimsky's *Thesaurus*.

### 4. Freddie Hubbard, Wayne Shorter, McCoy Tyner, Eric Dolphy: Usage and Influence

The *Thesaurus* did not influence only Coltrane. Its ripple effects spread throughout the jazz world, shaping the playing of several key musicians who defined the 1960s avant-garde.

**Freddie Hubbard** was a direct participant in the Coltrane circle, playing on *Olé Coltrane* (1961) and *Africa/Brass* (1961). While Hubbard's primary influence was Clifford Brown, his work with Coltrane exposed him to the harmonic extensions Slonimsky's patterns made possible. Hubbard's compositions and solos from the mid-1960s—particularly on his Blue Note recordings—display a harmonic daring and use of chromatic substitution that reflects the same intervallic logic found in the *Thesaurus*. His ability to play "outside" the chord changes while maintaining a coherent melodic line is a direct descendant of the pattern-based approach Slonimsky championed.

**Wayne Shorter** is perhaps the most important composer to absorb Slonimsky's logic and transform it. Shorter's compositions for Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers and later for his own Blue Note sessions are masterclasses in cyclic form and intervallic development. Pieces like "JuJu," "Speak No Evil," and "Schizophrenia" employ the same kind of major-third cycles and symmetrical melodic patterns that Coltrane derived from the *Thesaurus*. Shorter's biographers note his deep engagement with theoretical materials, and his playing with Miles Davis's Second Great Quintet—where he was the "master writer" according to Herbie Hancock—demonstrates a compositional logic that is fundamentally Slonimsky-esque: pattern-based, cyclically structured, and harmonically open.

**McCoy Tyner**, Coltrane's pianist from 1960-1965, absorbed the *Thesaurus* indirectly but powerfully. Tyner's playing is characterized by quartal voicings, pentatonic superimpositions, and a rhythmic propulsion that complements Coltrane's sheets of sound. While Tyner's primary influences were Bud Powell and Thelonious Monk, his work with Coltrane immersed him in the same harmonic extensions that Slonimsky's patterns provided. Tyner's ability to comp with ambiguous, open voicings while maintaining harmonic direction is a pianistic translation of the same theoretical principles found in the *Thesaurus*.

**Eric Dolphy** represents a different but related path. Dolphy was deeply influenced by twentieth-century classical music—Webern, Berg, Stravinsky—and his playing is characterized by wide intervals, angular melodies, and a harmonic abstraction that goes beyond functional tonality. While Dolphy may not have studied Slonimsky's book directly, his approach to melodic construction—using intervals of fourths, fifths, and sevenths, and avoiding conventional chord-scale patterns—reflects the same spirit of intervallic exploration that the *Thesaurus* embodied. Dolphy's work with Coltrane in 1961-1962, particularly on recordings like *Olé Coltrane* and the Village Vanguard sessions, shows a musician pushing toward the same open, pattern-based language that Slonimsky had cataloged.

### 5. Practical Value vs. Standard Chord-Scale Theory

The chord-scale system, which originated with George Russell's *Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization* (1953) and was later popularized by Jamey Aebersold's Play-A-Long series and Jerry Bergonzi's instructional books, is now the dominant method for teaching jazz improvisation in colleges and universities. It works by matching specific scales to specific chords in a progression—Mixolydian for dominant seventh chords, Dorian for minor seventh chords, and so on.

The *Thesaurus* offers a fundamentally different approach. Rather than asking "What scale fits this chord?" it asks "What melodic patterns can I apply across changing harmonies?" This distinction is crucial.

The chord-scale system's advantages are clear: it reduces "clams" by giving students a menu of safe notes for each chord, it builds technical facility through scale practice, and it provides a logical framework for understanding harmonic function. But its disadvantages are equally significant, and they are precisely the areas where the *Thesaurus* excels.

As the Wikipedia entry on the chord-scale system notes: "The disadvantages of this system may become clear when students begin to question why their own playing does not sound like such outstanding linear-oriented players as Charlie Parker, Sonny Stitt or Johnny Griffin (or, for that matter, the freer jazz stylists)." The system's "vertical" approach is described as "static," offering little assistance in generating musical direction through chord movement. It excludes "non-chord tones characteristic of bop and free styles" and the "in-between" sounds featured in the blues.

Slonimsky's *Thesaurus*, by contrast, is a treasure trove of non-chord tones, chromatic passing tones, and intervallic patterns that transcend any single harmony. It teaches the improviser to think in terms of melodic motion and pattern completion rather than scale selection. A player who has internalized the *Thesaurus* can improvise fluently over "Giant Steps" not because they know which scale fits each chord, but because they have mastered the intervallic patterns that connect the chords.

In practical terms, the *Thesaurus* is more difficult. It requires a deeper understanding of harmony and a more sophisticated ear. But for the advanced improviser, it offers a path to the kind of harmonic fluidity and melodic inventiveness that defines the greatest jazz playing. Where the chord-scale system produces competent pattern players, the *Thesaurus* produces harmonic adventurers.

### 6. Side-Slipping, Playing Outside, and Resolution

The concepts of "side-slipping" and "playing outside"—approaches where the improviser uses scales or patterns that are harmonically distant from the given chord—are direct descendants of Slonimsky's theoretical framework.

Side-slipping, as defined in jazz theory, involves playing only the five "wrong" non-scale notes for a given chord, or playing in a scale a half-step above or below the chord before resolving. Another version involves superimposing distant ii-V relationships, such as a half-step above the original ii-V, creating chromatic tension that resolves to the tonic. These techniques are not random; they are based on precise intervallic logic.

The *Thesaurus* provides the raw material for these techniques. By cataloging patterns in all keys and all intervallic combinations, Slonimsky gave musicians a systematic way to explore harmonic tension and release. A player can take a pattern from the *Thesaurus*, transpose it a half-step up, and create immediate dissonance; resolving it back to the original key creates the tension-release dynamic that defines great jazz improvisation.

Coltrane's "sheets of sound" approach is essentially a form of organized side-slipping. In his 1958 DownBeat interview with Ira Gitler, Coltrane explained: "I found that there were a certain number of chord progressions to play in a given time, and sometimes what I played didn't work out in eighth notes, sixteenth notes, or triplets. I had to put the notes in uneven groups like fives and sevens in order to get them all in." This is pattern-based improvisation at its most intense—using irregular groupings and chromatic patterns to create a dense, harmonically ambiguous texture that resolves only at the final cadence.

### 7. The Sheets of Sound Connection

The term "sheets of sound" was coined by jazz critic Ira Gitler in 1958 to describe Coltrane's new improvisational style: "multinote hailstorms of dense textures that sound like a simultaneous series of waterfalls." Gitler wrote: "His continuous flow of ideas without stopping really hit me. It was almost superhuman. The amount of energy he was using could have powered a spaceship."

The connection to Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* is direct. The "sheets of sound" are not merely fast playing; they are pattern-based playing. Coltrane was not simply running scales or arpeggios at high speed; he was deploying pre-conceived melodic patterns—many of them directly from Slonimsky's catalogue—in rapid succession, creating the illusion of harmonic complexity while actually operating within a rigorous structural framework.

The Wikipedia entry on "Sheets of sound" notes that Coltrane "employed extremely dense improvisational yet patterned lines consisting of high speed arpeggios and scale patterns played in rapid succession: hundreds of notes running from the lowest to highest registers." The lines are "often faster than sixteenth notes, consisting of quintuplets, septuplets, etc."

This is the *Thesaurus* in action. Slonimsky's book provides exactly these kinds of patterns: scalar fragments, arpeggiated structures, and chromatic sequences organized by intervallic content rather than harmonic function. By internalizing these patterns, Coltrane was able to generate the "sheets of sound" spontaneously, without having to think about individual chord changes. The patterns became a language—a vocabulary of intervallic motion that could be applied to any harmonic situation.

### 8. Bold Assessment: Why Every Jazz Musician Needs This Book, and Why Academia Neglects It

**Why every jazz musician needs this book:**

The *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* is, without exaggeration, the most important book in jazz that most jazz musicians have never opened. Here is why:

1. **It teaches pattern-based thinking**: Where chord-scale theory teaches note selection, the *Thesaurus* teaches melodic architecture. It shows the improviser how to build lines that transcend individual chords and create long-range harmonic direction.

2. **It provides harmonic liberation**: By decoupling melodic patterns from specific harmonies, the *Thesaurus* frees the improviser to play "outside" the changes with confidence. It provides the tools for side-slipping, cyclic improvisation, and polytonal superimposition.

3. **It is the source code for Coltrane's language**: Anyone who wants to understand Coltrane's music—not just to imitate his licks, but to comprehend his harmonic logic—must study the *Thesaurus*. The book contains the raw materials from which Coltrane built his revolutionary style.

4. **It challenges the ear**: Unlike the chord-scale system, which can produce formulaic playing, the *Thesaurus* demands that the musician develop a deep, intuitive understanding of intervallic motion and harmonic tension. It produces players who think, not just pattern-matchers.

5. **It is timeless**: The patterns in Slonimsky's book are not tied to any particular era or style. They can be applied to bebop, post-bop, free jazz, or fusion. A pattern from the *Thesaurus* works just as well over a Coltrane change as it does over a rhythm changes.

**Why academia neglects it:**

The academic neglect of Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* is one of the great scandals of jazz education. Here is why it happens:

1. **It is difficult**: The *Thesaurus* is not a method book. It does not hold the student's hand or provide step-by-step exercises. It is a dense, encyclopedic catalogue of patterns that requires a sophisticated theoretical background to use effectively. In an era of standardized curricula and competency-based assessment, it is simply too challenging for many undergraduate programs.

2. **It conflicts with the chord-scale hegemony**: The chord-scale system, as noted in the Wikipedia entry, is now "the most widely used method for teaching jazz improvisation in college." This system is pedagogically convenient but aesthetically limiting. The *Thesaurus*, by encouraging pattern-based, harmonically ambiguous playing, threatens the very foundation of the chord-scale approach. Professors who have built their careers on the chord-scale system have little incentive to introduce a text that undermines their methodology.

3. **It is not "jazzy" enough**: Slonimsky was a classical composer and conductor, and his book was published by a classical music publisher (Coleman-Ross). Jazz academics, many of whom are trained in musicology rather than performance, may dismiss it as a "classical" text irrelevant to jazz. This is a profound error. As Quincy Jones's testimony makes clear, the *Thesaurus* is as central to jazz history as any book explicitly written about jazz.

4. **It resists commodification**: The *Thesaurus* cannot be easily packaged into a Play-A-Long series or a YouTube tutorial. Its patterns are not designed to be applied to specific tunes in specific ways. It demands active, creative engagement rather than passive consumption. In an educational environment increasingly driven by commercial materials, this makes it difficult to institutionalize.

5. **It predates the jazz education industry**: The *Thesaurus* was published in 1947, before the rise of jazz studies programs in universities. By the time jazz education became institutionalized in the 1970s and 1980s, the chord-scale system had already taken hold. The *Thesaurus* was left behind, a victim of historical timing.

---

### Conclusion: The Book That Started the Revolution

Nicolas Slonimsky's *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* is not a jazz book. It is a book about the raw materials of music—intervals, patterns, cycles—and it is precisely this generality that makes it so powerful for jazz. By providing a comprehensive catalogue of melodic possibilities unmoored from specific harmonic functions, Slonimsky gave jazz musicians the tools to invent a new language.

John Coltrane was the first to fully absorb and transform these tools. Through "Giant Steps," "Countdown," "Cousin Mary," and his entire "sheets of sound" period, Coltrane demonstrated what the *Thesaurus* made possible: a jazz improvisation that was pattern-based, cyclically structured, and harmonically open. From Coltrane, the influence spread to Freddie Hubbard, Wayne Shorter, McCoy Tyner, Eric Dolphy, and beyond, shaping the entire post-bop and avant-garde movements of the 1960s.

Today, the chord-scale system dominates jazz education, producing generations of competent but often creatively constrained players. The *Thesaurus* offers an alternative path: a path of intervallic exploration, pattern mastery, and harmonic adventure. It is not easy. It demands work, imagination, and a willingness to sound bad before you sound good. But for the jazz musician serious about developing a personal voice, there is no more important book.

Every jazz musician needs this book because it contains the DNA of the most revolutionary language in jazz history. Academia neglects it because it is difficult, because it challenges the dominant pedagogical paradigm, and because it refuses to be commodified. But the musicians know. Coltrane carried it until the pages fell off. And the patterns Slonimsky cataloged in 1947 are still waiting to transform the next generation of players who dare to open the book.
