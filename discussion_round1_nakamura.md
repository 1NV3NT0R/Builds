# Response to Dr. Elena Voronina and Marcus Webb
## On Nicolas Slonimsky's *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* (1947)

**By Kenji Nakamura**
*Composer and Guitarist*

---

## I. With Elena: On Theoretical Depth and Historical Significance

Elena, your report anchors the *Thesaurus* in precisely the intellectual and biographical soil from which it grew, and I want to affirm your central claim: Slonimsky's book is not merely a pattern book. It is a *theoretical event*—a systematic intervention in the history of pitch organization that deserves to be read alongside Schoenberg's *Harmonielehre*, Messiaen's *Technique de mon langage musical*, and Lewin's *Generalized Musical Intervals and Transformations*. Your unpacking of the equal-division principle—tracing it from the tritone through the major-third cycle and beyond to the eleven-octave divisions—correctly identifies this as the spine of the entire project. You are right to emphasize that Slonimsky did not simply catalogue exotic scales; he revealed the combinatorial grammar that generates them.

I also want to endorse your treatment of Slonimsky's terminology. You handle the neologisms with appropriate nuance: they are not mere pedantry but a deliberate attempt to construct an ideologically neutral metalanguage. As a composer working in a post-tonal idiom, I have felt this directly. When I write for guitar—an instrument freighted with the conceptual baggage of "scale positions" and "CAGED" systems rooted in major/minor tonality—Slonimsky's terms liberate me from the implicit hierarchy those names carry. "Sesquitone" does not sound like a borrowed chord; it sounds like a structural unit. Your biographical contextualization of this linguistic project is therefore not incidental but essential.

Your section on Messiaen is especially valuable. The near-simultaneity of Messiaen's *Technique* (1944) and Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* (1947) is, as you note, no coincidence. Both men were responding to the same crisis in postwar modernism: the exhaustion of functional tonality and the narrowing threat of serial orthodoxy. Where Messiaen responded with a mystical, coloristic catalogue of "modes of limited transposition," Slonimsky responded with a combinatorial, almost engineering-minded manual of equal divisions. The two books complement and complicate each other. Messiaen gives us the spiritual aura of symmetric scales; Slonimsky gives us their generative mathematics. Your bringing these two into dialogue corrects the tendency to treat the *Thesaurus* as an isolated curiosity.

Similarly, your Bartók connection is well-placed. Slonimsky's personal relationship with Bartók—the Paris première of the First Piano Concerto—is not merely biographical trivia; it marks a point of transmission between Bartók's intuitive use of interval cycles and Slonimsky's systematization of them. I would push this connection even further than you do. Bartók's *Concerto for Orchestra*, with its notorious "Night Music" movements built on perfect-fourth cycles and its finale built on a D–C♯–D–C pedal-point, exhibits precisely the kind of intervortic logic that Slonimsky made explicit. What Bartók deployed as a compositional intuition, Slonimsky converted into a teachable method. Your analysis of this lineage is accurate, and I hope future scholarship follows your lead in treating Bartók as a direct precursor to the *Thesaurus* rather than as a parallel figure.

Where you are most indispensable, Elena, is in your insistence that the *Thesaurus* is both systematic and idiosyncratic. The mathematical logic you trace—cyclic groups, combinatorial structures, the anticipation of set-theoretic operations—is real. But so is Slonimsky's refusal to formalize that logic in the austere manner of a Babbitt or a Forte. The *Thesaurus* is systematic but not systematic *only*. It is a grimoire as much as a textbook. Your ability to hold both of these truths simultaneously—rigor and idiosyncrasy, the mathematical and the magical—is what makes your report an important piece of scholarship in its own right.

## II. With Marcus: On Coltrane, the Sheets of Sound, and the Jazz Revolution

Marcus, your report made me put down my guitar and pick up the *Thesaurus* with new ears. Your insistence that the book's central value is not harmonic but *intervallic*—that Slonimsky gives us melodic motion rather than chord-scale matching—is, I think, the most important interpretive claim one can make about this material from a jazz perspective. You are absolutely right: the chord-scale system is a theory of vertical selection (which scale over which chord?), while the *Thesaurus* is a theory of horizontal trajectory (which pattern connects which points in pitch space?). This distinction is not minor; it is the difference between playing *on* the changes and playing *through* them.

Your documentation of Coltrane's relationship to the book is compelling. The Quincy Jones testimony—"Everything that Coltrane ever played was in that thesaurus"—is explosive, and your willingness to let it hang in the air, untidily, is a credit to your intellectual honesty. You do not oversimplify the causal chain. You acknowledge that Coltrane absorbed the patterns, internalized them, and then transformed them through his own harmonic imagination. The distinction between "Giant Steps" as a direct transcription of a Slonimsky pattern and "Giant Steps" as Coltrane's own synthesis of Slonimsky-esque logic is subtle but crucial, and you navigate it well.

Your analysis of "Countdown" is particularly illuminating. The reharmonization of "Tune Up" through rapid chromatic third-relations is a perfect example of what Slonimsky makes possible: not the mere superimposition of "wrong" scales, but the structural reorganization of the progression's intervallic DNA. When Coltrane substitutes a D–F–A♭ arpeggio where a D minor seventh chord would normally sit, he is not just playing "outside"; he is applying the logic of the ditone progression with infrapolation. You correctly identify this as pattern-based playing, not random dissonance.

I want to extend your argument about "sheets of sound" in a direction your report gestures toward but does not fully develop. The sheets are not merely fast. They are *metric*—they are organized around irregular groupings (fives, sevens, nines) that Slonimsky himself explores in his discussions of polymetric and polyrhythmic scales (chapters 34–36, in your terminology). When Coltrane plays a septuplet cascade over a 4/4 bar, he is creating a temporary metric dislocation that resolves only at the bar line. This is not unlike what Slonimsky describes when he discusses "crossing intervals" and the superposition of different cyclic divisions. The sheets of sound are, in this sense, *metric Slonimsky* as much as they are harmonic Slonimsky. Your analysis of the technique as "organized side-slipping" is excellent, but I would push further: the sheets are organized *temporally* as well as *harmonically*.

Your section on Freddie Hubbard, Wayne Shorter, McCoy Tyner, and Eric Dolphy is strong, though I think you understate Wayne Shorter's case. Shorter's compositions from 1964–1968—"JuJu," "Speak No Evil," "Schizophrenia," "Nefertiti"—do not merely "employ the same kind of major-third cycles" Coltrane derived from Slonimsky. They *systematize* them into a compositional logic that is independent of improvisation. Shorter's melodies are themselves *Thesaurus*-style patterns, embedded in formal structures that make the cyclic logic explicit. Herbie Hancock's testimony that Shorter was the "master writer" of the Miles Davis Quintet is, in retrospect, testimony to Shorter's Slonimsky-esque compositional method. I would have liked to see you develop this point further, as it represents one of the most important extensions of Slonimsky's influence beyond improvisation into composed jazz.

Your polemic against chord-scale theory and its institutional capture of jazz education is necessary and brave. The Wikipedia entry you cite—describing the chord-scale system as "static" and ill-equipped to produce players like Parker or Coltrane—is damning, and your diagnosis of the institutional reasons for the *Thesaurus*'s neglect is convincing. I would add one reason of my own: the chord-scale system is *teachable* in ways the *Thesaurus* is not. It can be broken into discrete, testable competencies. The *Thesaurus* requires synthesis, intuition, and risk. In a competency-based educational environment, it is poison. Your point about commodification is equally important. The *Thesaurus* cannot be reduced to a Play-A-Long track or a YouTube lick lesson. It demands that the musician become a composer of patterns, not a consumer of them.

## III. Pushbacks, Corrections, and Missing Angles

Having affirmed the immense value of both reports, I want to turn to areas where I disagree, where I think claims are overstated, or where a contemporary compositional perspective reveals something both of you have missed.

### 1. On Quincy Jones and the "Giant Steps" Attribution

Marcus, I must push back gently on your quotation of Quincy Jones: "It's Slonimsky. That book started all the jazz guys improvising in 12-tone." This is, I think, a conflation of two distinct things: the *Thesaurus*'s cyclic patterns and twelve-tone serialism. The *Thesaurus* contains twelve-tone constructions—Slonimsky calls them "dodecaphonic patterns"—but these are not the source of Coltrane's harmonic language in "Giant Steps." Coltrane's changes are not serial; they are *cyclotonal*. They operate within the语法 of functional tonality—ii–V–I progressions—while displacing the tonal centers by major thirds. This is a different operation from serial ordering. The *Thesaurus* gives Coltrane the interval cycle; it does not give him the row. To say that "Giant Steps" is Slonimsky's composition, as Jones implies, is to confuse generative material with compositional realization. Coltrane's genius was precisely to embed Slonimsky's intervallic logic inside a jazz harmonic syntax. The *Thesaurus* provided the raw material; Coltrane provided the syntax.

Elena, I want to make a related pushback to your claim that Slonimsky "arrived at" the same cyclic progression as Schoenberg "precisely, note for note, with no connection whatsoever." The anecdote is charming, but it risks mystifying the relationship between the two systems. Schoenberg's twelve-tone rows and Slonimsky's interval cycles are not the same thing, even when they produce similar pitch content. A ditone progression generated by repeating the major third is a *cyclic* operation; a twelve-tone row is a *permutational* operation. They can produce overlapping results, but their generative logics are distinct. Your report treats this distinction with appropriate care elsewhere, but this particular anecdote could mislead readers into thinking that serialism and Slonimsky's method are interchangeable, when in fact they represent two different modernist paradigms that occasionally converge.

### 2. On the "Complete Systematic" Nature of the *Thesaurus*

Elena, your report repeatedly describes the *Thesaurus* as "systematic" and "rigorous." I want to complicate this. The book is systematic in its mathematical foundations, but it is not systematic in its *realization*. The 1,300+ patterns are not exhaustively derived from a closed algorithm in the way a serial row is derived from a combinatorial matrix. Slonimsky selected patterns for their musical utility, their playability, and their coloristic interest. There are patterns in the *Thesaurus* that are musically trivial—generated by mechanical repetition of an interval without regard for melodic contour—and there are patterns of extraordinary invention. The selection process is *curatorial*, not purely algorithmic. As a composer, I find this to be one of the book's greatest strengths, but it also means that the *Thesaurus* is not the "comprehensive catalogue of all possibilities" Slonimsky sometimes claimed it to be. It is a *curated* catalogue, shaped by Slonimsky's taste as a performer and his ear as a listener. Your report could have explored this tension between mathematical exhaustiveness and musical selection more explicitly.

### 3. On the Missing Contemporary Compositional Angle

Both reports, in different ways, treat the *Thesaurus* as either a historical document (Elena) or as a source for jazz improvisation (Marcus). Neither of you engages seriously with what the *Thesaurus* means for *contemporary composition* today. This is, I think, the most significant gap in both reports.

I am a composer working in 2026. The *Thesaurus* is not a historical curiosity to me; it is a live compositional resource. Here is what both of you miss:

**a) The *Thesaurus* as a tool for algorithmic and computer-assisted composition.** Slonimsky's combinatorial logic is directly translatable into code. A composer working with SuperCollider, Max/MSP, or even Python can generate Slonimsky-esque patterns algorithmically, combine them with other pitch collections, and explore parameter spaces that Slonimsky could only imagine. The *Thesaurus* is, in effect, a pre-digital generative system. Neither of you treats it this way.

**b) The *Thesaurus* and microtonality.** Slonimsky worked in twelve-tone equal temperament, but his interval-cycling logic is agnostic to tuning. A composer working with just intonation, microtonal equal temperaments (19, 31, 53), or spectral techniques can apply the *Thesaurus*'s principle of equal division to *any* division of the octave. The result is a family of "Slonimsky-like" scales in tunings that Slonimsky never imagined. This is not a footnote; it is a frontier.

**c) The *Thesaurus* and timbre.** Both reports treat the *Thesaurus* as a pitch resource. But Slonimsky's patterns are also *registral* and *textural* resources. The way a ditone progression spans multiple octaves creates a specific kind of registral hierarchy—low, sparse notes alternating with high, dense ones—that is directly relevant to composers thinking about spatialization and orchestration. Marcus, this is particularly relevant to your point about sheets of sound: the *Thesaurus* is not just about which notes to play, but about *how they cascade across the instrument's range*.

**d) The *Thesaurus* and post-genre practice.** You both treat the *Thesaurus* as a bridge between classical and jazz. But in 2026, that bridge has become a highway. Composers like Tyshawn Sorey, Vijay Iyer, and Mary Halvorson—all of whom I count as peers and influences—work in a space where "classical" and "jazz" are not distinct traditions but overlapping resources. The *Thesaurus* fits this post-genre world perfectly because it was never exclusively either. Your reports capture this, but they do not push it far enough.

### 4. On Marcus's Claim about the Chord-Scale System

Marcus, your critique of chord-scale theory is largely justified, but I want to push back on one point. You suggest that the chord-scale system produces "competent but often creatively constrained players." This is true, but it is not the whole story. The chord-scale system is a *pedagogical scaffold*—it gets beginners from "I don't know what notes to play" to "I can outline a ii–V–I." The problem is not the system itself but its *institutionalization as an endpoint* rather than a starting point. The most creative jazz musicians I know—including many who use chord-scale language—treat it as a base camp from which to climb into Slonimsky-esque territory. Your report risks throwing out the scaffold because some people never climb the mountain. The solution is not to abandon chord-scale theory but to insist that it is a *means*, not an *end*.

## IV. What I Learned: Enriching My Practice as Composer and Guitarist

Both reports have fundamentally altered how I hear and write music. Let me be specific.

**From Elena:** Your insistence on the mathematical structure behind the patterns gave me a new compositional tool. I have spent the last month exploring the *Thesaurus* not as a pattern book but as a *generative system*. I wrote a short piece for solo guitar using only the sesquitone (minor third) progression with interpolation and infrapolation. The result was a piece that sounds harmonically ambiguous yet structurally rigorous—exactly the quality Slonimsky was after. Your historical contextualization also made me hear Messiaen differently. I went back to the *Turangalîla-Symphonie* and heard the *Thesaurus*-like patterns in the "love themes" with new ears.

**From Marcus:** Your analysis of Coltrane's "sheets of sound" as organized side-slipping gave me a new way to practice. I have been taking Slonimsky patterns, transposing them a half-step above the tonic, and practicing resolving them back into the diatonic collection. The tension-release effect is extraordinary. More importantly, your critique of chord-scale theory has made me rethink my own teaching. I teach guitar at a conservatory, and I have been using chord-scale systems with my students. After reading your report, I restructured my curriculum: I now introduce the *Thesaurus* early, not as a method book but as a *laboratory* for intervallic exploration. The results have been striking—my students are playing with more harmonic risk and more melodic originality.

Both of you have also deepened my understanding of Slonimsky as a *performer's theorist*. Elena highlights his biography as conductor and pianist; Marcus highlights his influence on improvisers. Together, they reveal that Slonimsky was writing from the perspective of someone who needed patterns that could be executed in real time. This is not a trivial point. The *Thesaurus* is full of patterns that are awkward to read but natural to play—patterns that exploit the ergonomics of keyboard and string instruments in ways that purely theoretical systems do not. As a guitarist, I feel this in my fingers.

## V. A Bold Synthesis: Three Perspectives, One Revelation

If I were to synthesize what Elena's historical-theoretical perspective, Marcus's jazz-practical perspective, and my own compositional perspective reveal together, it would be this:

**The *Thesaurus* is not a book about scales. It is a book about *musical time*.**

Let me explain. Elena gives us the mathematical architecture—the interval cycles, the symmetrical divisions, the combinatorial logic. This architecture is spatial: it maps out pitch space as a set of relationships. Marcus gives us the temporal realization—the Coltrane changes, the sheets of sound, the side-slipping. This realization is temporal: it maps out how those spatial relationships unfold in the flow of improvisation. What neither of you fully articulates—because your perspectives are, respectively, historical and performative—is that the *Thesaurus*'s deepest contribution is to show how *space* and *time* in music are generated from the same intervallic source.

When Slonimsky divides the octave into twelve equal semitones, he creates a *spatial* map of the chromatic universe. When Coltrane takes that map and generates a "Giant Steps" progression from it, he creates a *temporal* journey through that universe. The pattern is the same; its deployment is different. But the *Thesaurus* reveals that the spatial and the temporal are not opposed. They are two manifestations of the same intervallic logic.

From a compositional perspective, this is revolutionary. It means that the decision about *which notes to play* (the spatial question) is simultaneously a decision about *how long to play them and in what order* (the temporal question). In traditional tonal music, these decisions are governed by functional harmony—the chord progression dictates both pitch content and temporal sequence. In Slonimsky's system, the intervallic cycle generates both simultaneously. A ditone progression is both a harmonic structure (spatial) and a melodic trajectory (temporal). This is why it works so well for Coltrane: it collapses the distinction between harmony and melody, between vertical and horizontal, that chord-scale theory preserves.

This synthesis reveals something none of us could see alone. Elena's historical perspective shows us the *Thesaurus* as a product of its time—a response to the crisis of tonality and the challenge of serialism. Marcus's jazz perspective shows us the *Thesaurus* as a source of creative liberation—a tool for escaping the constraints of functional harmony and chord-scale thinking. My compositional perspective shows us the *Thesaurus* as a *generative grammar*—a system that produces not just patterns but *temporal architectures*.

Together, we see that the *Thesaurus* is a *unified field theory* of modernist pitch organization. It does not choose between the spatial logic of set theory and the temporal logic of progression; it shows that both arise from the same intervallic root. This is Slonimsky's deepest insight, and it is an insight that remains underexplored in both musicology and jazz studies.

For me, as a composer, this means that the *Thesaurus* is not a resource I dip into when I need an exotic scale. It is a *method*—a way of generating entire compositions from a single intervallic principle. My piece for solo guitar mentioned earlier was not built by selecting patterns from the *Thesaurus*; it was built by applying the *Thesaurus*'s generative logic—equal division, interpolation, infrapolation—to a single seed interval and letting the structure unfold from there. This is what Slonimsky intended, and it is what both of your reports, in different ways, make possible.

Elena, your report gave me the historical and theoretical vocabulary to understand what I was doing. Marcus, your report gave me the practical and improvisational vocabulary to hear what it could sound like. Together, you have given me the confidence to treat the *Thesaurus* not as a museum piece but as a living compositional technology.

To both of you: thank you. The *Thesaurus* is richer for your having written about it, and the conversation we are now having is precisely the kind of interdisciplinary dialogue—between musicology, performance, and composition—that Slonimsky himself would have relished. He was, after all, a man who believed that all twelve tones were equal, that all musical perspectives were valid, and that the future of music lay in the free and open exploration of possibility. Our three reports, converging on a single book from three distinct angles, are the best tribute we could offer to that belief.
