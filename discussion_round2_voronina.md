## I. Introduction: A Conversation That Transforms the Object of Study

To engage with Marcus Webb's and Kenji Nakamura's Round 1 reports is to witness the Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns become, before our eyes, a richer and more complex object than any single one of us could have imagined. Marcus has reminded us that the book lives not only in libraries but in sweat-drenched basement practice rooms, in the calluses of saxophonists and the pencil-marked pages of gig-ready musicians. Kenji has shown us that the book's logic transcends genre, instrument, and even century, speaking directly to algorithmic composition, spectral technique, and the post-genre condition of contemporary music. Both reports have forced me to revise, refine, and in some cases fundamentally reconsider my own positions. What follows is my scholarly response, addressed to both colleagues with the respect and intellectual honesty that this extraordinary conversation demands.

---

## II. On Marcus's Pushbacks: Reception History, Anecdote, and the Embodied Dimension

### A. Academic Neglect and the Jazz Underground

Marcus pushes back against my claim that the Thesaurus was "largely ignored" for fifteen years, and he is right to do so—though with an important qualification. When I wrote those words, I was describing the book's reception within the classical music establishment: the conservatories, the university curricula, the critical journals, and the institutional networks that defined "legitimate" musical knowledge in the 1950s and 1960s. In that context, the neglect is documented and severe. The Thesaurus received virtually no reviews in major musicological publications, was absent from the syllabi of leading conservatories, and was not cited in the theoretical literature of the period. The institutional memory of classical music was, for decades, profoundly amnesiac about Slonimsky's encyclopedic achievement.

But Marcus is correct that this narrative obscures a parallel history of active appropriation. The jazz community of the 1950s and 1960s—an oral culture operating through basement rehearsals, after-hours sessions, and the informal networks that Marcus describes—did not "ignore" the Thesaurus. It discovered it, used it, and transformed it with astonishing speed. Marcus's testimony that the book was "simply not yet available" to jazz musicians in the late 1940s is historically accurate: distribution channels for the book were limited, and the jazz community relied on the same postal networks and personal contacts that disseminated Charlie Parker solos and Thelonious Monk voicings. Within a decade of publication, however, the Thesaurus was circulating among serious musicians, not as a museum piece but as a living tool. I welcome Marcus's correction and will revise my reception history to distinguish more carefully between *academic* neglect and *jazz community* appropriation. These are not the same phenomenon, and conflating them does a disservice to both histories.

### B. The Quincy Jones Anecdote: Anecdotal Power and Documentary Limits

I must clarify a point that may have generated confusion: in my Round 1 report, I already pushed back against Quincy Jones's 2018 testimony that the Thesaurus contained the exact pattern of "Giant Steps" "right near the front." I wrote that this was "anecdotal rather than documentary" and that comparative analysis by Jeff Bair found structural similarities but not exact matches. Marcus, however, seems to treat the anecdote as validated by his own lived experience of the jazz world: "When Quincy Jones says he used to see Coltrane with the book, he is describing a practice that was already widespread among serious jazz musicians by the late 1950s."

I agree with Marcus about the widespread practice, but I maintain my skepticism about the specific content of Jones's claim. The anecdote that the Thesaurus "started all the jazz guys improvising in 12-tone" conflates two distinct operations: Slonimsky's cyclic pattern generation and Schoenberg's twelve-tone serialism. Coltrane's language in "Giant Steps" is cyclotonal, not serial. It operates within the syntax of functional tonality—ii–V–I progressions—while displacing tonal centers by major thirds. The Thesaurus provided the *interval cycle*; Coltrane provided the *harmonic syntax*. To call Coltrane's music "12-tone" is to mischaracterize both composers and to flatten the distinction between Slonimsky's combinatorial method and Schoenberg's permutational method—precisely the distinction that Kenji correctly emphasizes in his report.

Marcus is right that anecdotal testimony has its own kind of truth. Jones's recollection reflects a lived reality: Coltrane did carry the Thesaurus, did study it intensively, and did incorporate its patterns into his playing. But the specific claim that the book contains a "12-tone example" that is "Giant Steps" has not been verified by close comparative analysis. We must be careful not to let dramatic anecdotes substitute for rigorous scholarship, even when those anecdotes reflect genuine historical practices.

### C. "Source Code" vs. "Catalyst": A Metaphor Revised

I must correct a possible misreading of my Round 1 position. In that report, I explicitly rejected the "source code" metaphor, writing that the book was "a catalyst, not a source code." Marcus appears to accept this framing, but I want to ensure my position is unambiguous: the Thesaurus provided Coltrane with a vocabulary of intervallic motion—a set of raw materials—but it was Coltrane's genius to synthesize these materials with his other influences into something wholly original. The danger of the "source code" metaphor is that it implies deterministic transmission: input Slonimsky, output Coltrane. The reality is mediated transformation: Coltrane absorbed Slonimsky's patterns, internalized them through years of practice, and then reconfigured them through his own harmonic imagination, his spiritual practice, and his rhythmic innovation.

Marcus's contribution is to show us what this transformation looks like from the inside: the physical practice of internalizing patterns, the bandstand reality of executing them at 320 BPM, the sweat and risk of making them communicate emotion to a living audience. I am grateful for this reminder that the Thesaurus is not merely a theoretical document but a *practical* one—a tool that must be embodied to be effective.

### D. Democratic Pitch Collection: Theory vs. Practice

Marcus's pushback on the "democratic pitch collection" claim is astute and requires revision of my position. I wrote that the Thesaurus embodies a "democratic" approach in which all twelve tones are equal. Marcus correctly observes that in jazz practice, not all twelve tones are equal: the major third and major seventh retain tonal gravity, the tritone still resolves, and "wrong" notes are heard as wrong until they are resolved through appropriate voice leading.

I need to refine my claim. The Thesaurus's democratic ethos is *structural*, not *perceptual*. Slonimsky's mathematical framework treats all twelve pitch classes as equivalent units in a combinatorial system—this is the democratic claim. But the *use* of that system in jazz necessarily re-introduces hierarchical relations: the tension between diatonic and non-diatonic, between resolution and delay, is precisely what gives jazz its narrative drive. The Thesaurus does not abolish tonality; it expands it. It provides the materials for creating tension and release, not for eliminating them. Marcus is right that if all twelve tones were truly equal in perceptual effect, jazz would lose its emotional power. The democratic claim holds at the level of *generative possibility*, not at the level of *aesthetic effect*.

### E. Physicality: The Embodied Dimension of Pattern Mastery

Both Kenji and I, in our Round 1 reports, treated the Thesaurus primarily as a conceptual or theoretical document. Marcus's insistence on its physicality is a crucial corrective. The Thesaurus is not merely a catalogue of pitch collections; it is a *method book*—a set of exercises that must be executed with breath, fingers, embouchure, and diaphragm. When Marcus practices pattern #286—the ditone progression with infra-interpolation—he is not just analyzing an intervallic structure. He is moving his fingers in specific patterns across the saxophone keys, breathing in specific rhythmic relationships, shaping his tone to produce the legato phrases that Coltrane made famous.

This embodied dimension is not incidental to the Thesaurus's meaning; it is constitutive of it. Slonimsky was a performer as well as a theorist, and his patterns reflect the ergonomics of keyboard and string instruments in ways that purely theoretical systems do not. The Thesaurus is full of patterns that are awkward to read but natural to play—patterns that exploit the physical affordances of specific instruments. Marcus's contribution is to remind us that the book's value lies not only in what it says but in what it *does* to the musician's body and mind through sustained practice.

## III. On Kenji's Pushbacks: Systematicity, Anecdote, and the Temporal Turn

### A. The Schoenberg Anecdote and the Cyclic/Permutational Distinction

Kenji pushes back on my claim that Slonimsky "arrived at the same cyclic progression as Schoenberg 'precisely, note for note, with no connection whatsoever.'" I need to clarify what I actually argued and where my presentation may have been misleading. In my Round 1 report, I discussed the well-known anecdote that Slonimsky and Schoenberg independently discovered the same ditone progression—a pattern based on dividing the octave into major thirds. I used this anecdote to illustrate what I called a "convergence" rather than an "influence": two composers working from different methodological starting points arrived at similar pitch collections.

Kenji is right that the anecdote risks mystifying the relationship between the two systems. A ditone progression generated by repeating the major third is a *cyclic* operation; a twelve-tone row is a *permutational* operation. They can produce overlapping pitch content, but their generative logics are distinct. I should have emphasized this distinction more carefully. The anecdote is not wrong, but it can mislead if presented as evidence that serialism and Slonimsky's method are interchangeable. They are not. They represent two different modernist paradigms that occasionally converge in their results while maintaining distinct operational logics. I welcome Kenji's correction and will revise my presentation of the anecdote accordingly.

### B. Exhaustive Systematic Derivation: The Curatorial Imperative

Kenji's pushback against my characterization of the Thesaurus as "systematic" and "rigorous" is well-taken. I did describe the book as systematic, and I did emphasize its mathematical foundations. But Kenji correctly points out that the 1,300+ patterns are not exhaustively derived from a closed algorithm in the way a serial row is derived from a combinatorial matrix. Slonimsky selected patterns for their musical utility, their playability, and their coloristic interest. There are patterns in the Thesaurus that are musically trivial—generated by mechanical repetition of an interval without regard for melodic contour—and there are patterns of extraordinary invention.

The selection process is curatorial, not purely algorithmic. This is not a weakness of the book; it is one of its greatest strengths. The Thesaurus is systematic in its mathematical foundations but idiosyncratic in its realization. Slonimsky was a performer and a listener as well as a mathematician, and his ear shaped the catalogue as much as his logic. My report could have explored this tension more explicitly, and I welcome Kenji's intervention. The Thesaurus is not a "comprehensive catalogue of all possibilities" in any strict mathematical sense; it is a curated catalogue shaped by Slonimsky's taste and his experience as a working musician. This curatorial dimension is what makes the book musically useful rather than merely mathematically complete.

### C. The "Musical Time" Synthesis: A Profound but Incomplete Insight

Kenji's bold synthesis—that the Thesaurus is "not a book about scales. It is a book about musical time"—is the most provocative claim in either Round 1 report. I want to engage with it seriously, because I think it is profoundly insightful but ultimately incomplete.

Kenji's argument is that the Thesaurus's mathematical architecture (the interval cycles, symmetrical divisions, combinatorial logic) maps out *pitch space* as a set of relationships, while Coltrane's realization of those patterns maps out *temporal* trajectories through that space. The Thesaurus reveals that space and time in music are generated from the same intervallic source.

This is an important insight, but it needs qualification. The Thesaurus is not primarily a theory of temporal organization. It does not specify rhythmic values, metric placements, or temporal proportions. It presents *pitch sequences*—melodic patterns—without rhythmic specification. The temporal dimension that Kenji identifies is added by the performer or composer who realizes these patterns in time. Coltrane's "sheets of sound" are indeed metric and temporal, but they are Coltrane's addition to Slonimsky's materials, not something inherent in the Thesaurus itself.

That said, Kenji is right that the Thesaurus contains the *potential* for temporal organization. The cyclic patterns that Slonimsky catalogues have an inherent periodicity—a ditone progression repeats the same intervallic structure at regular pitch intervals—and this periodicity can be realized temporally as rhythmic repetition or metric dislocation. The Thesaurus provides the *spatial* map; the performer provides the *temporal* journey. Kenji's insight is that these are not separate domains but two manifestations of the same intervallic logic. I accept this insight but want to emphasize that the temporal dimension is realized, not given, in the Thesaurus itself. The book contains the seeds of temporal organization, but those seeds require a performer or composer to germinate them.

### D. The Contemporary Compositional Dimension: A Gap Acknowledged

Kenji's push that both Marcus and I underemphasized the Thesaurus's relevance to contemporary composition is well-founded. I focused my Round 1 report on the book's historical context and its influence on jazz, while Kenji has shown that the book's generative logic is directly applicable to algorithmic composition, microtonality, timbre, and post-genre practice. I accept this expansion and will revise my framework to include the contemporary compositional dimension that Kenji has so vividly demonstrated. The Thesaurus is not merely a historical document or a jazz method book; it is a living compositional resource that speaks directly to the conditions of 21st-century musical practice.

## IV. New Convergences and Disagreements

### A. Convergences That Have Emerged

1. **Cross-genre portability**: All three of us now agree that the Thesaurus functions across classical, jazz, rock, and electronic music. Marcus emphasizes the jazz lineage, Kenji emphasizes the rock and electronic lineage, and I provide the classical modernist context. Together, we reveal the book as a boundary object that translates between traditions.

2. **Generative logic over fixed materials**: We all agree that the Thesaurus's value lies not in its specific patterns but in its *method* of pattern generation—the operations of infrapolation, ultrapolation, interpolation, and mirror inversion that transform simple intervals into complex melodic structures.

3. **Anti-dogmatic design**: We all recognize that Slonimsky's refusal to prescribe a single aesthetic or technical approach is the book's defining characteristic. It is a tool for creative liberation, not a method for enforcing orthodoxy.

4. **The embodied dimension**: Marcus has convinced both Kenji and me that the Thesaurus must be understood not only as a conceptual document but as a physical practice—a set of motor programs that must be internalized through repetition, breath, and sweat.

### B. Disagreements That Persist

1. **Primary vehicle**: Marcus argues that the saxophone tradition (Coltrane, Shorter, Brecker) is the primary vehicle for the Thesaurus's influence. Kenji argues that the guitar tradition (Holdsworth, Lane, Buckethead) is equally important. I think both are right, but I would add that the *conceptual* transmission—the idea of melody as pattern generation—transcends any single instrument. The Thesaurus's influence is instrument-agnostic because it operates at the level of intervallic logic.

2. **The nature of systematicity**: Kenji complicates my claim that the Thesaurus is "systematic" by pointing out its curatorial dimension. I accept this complication but want to maintain that the mathematical logic is real and constraining, even if the selection of patterns is idiosyncratic. The Thesaurus is both systematic and curatorial—these are not contradictory but complementary dimensions of the book's design.

3. **The temporal dimension**: Kenji argues that the Thesaurus is fundamentally about musical time. I accept that it contains the potential for temporal organization but maintain that its primary domain is pitch space. The temporal dimension is realized by performers and composers, not given in the book itself. This is a disagreement about emphasis rather than substance.

## V. New Insights and Revisions

### A. The Thesaurus as Boundary Object

Reading Marcus's and Kenji's reports has led me to a new conceptual framework: the Thesaurus as a *boundary object* in the sense described by sociologists of science like Susan Leigh Star and James Griesemer. A boundary object is something that can be used by different communities for different purposes while maintaining a common identity. The Thesaurus functions exactly this way: classical composers use it for systematic exploration of pitch collections, jazz musicians use it for intervallic fluency, rock guitarists use it for harmonic expansion, and electronic musicians use it for algorithmic generation. Each community reads the book differently, but all recognize it as the same object. This conceptual portability is not accidental; it is the result of Slonimsky's deliberate choice to present materials without prescribing their use. The Thesaurus is a Rosetta Stone that translates between musical traditions because it operates at a level of abstraction—intervallic logic—that transcends any specific style or genre.

### B. The Tension Between Democratic Ethos and Hierarchical Practice

Marcus's pushback on the "democratic pitch collection" claim has led me to refine my understanding of Slonimsky's democratic ethos. The Thesaurus's mathematical framework does treat all twelve tones as equal, but this equality is *structural* and *generative*, not *perceptual* or *aesthetic*. In practice, musicians re-introduce hierarchy through use: the major third retains tonal gravity, the tritone still resolves, and "wrong" notes are heard as wrong until they are resolved through appropriate voice leading. The democratic claim is about the *possibility* of musical thinking, not about the *effect* of musical thinking. Slonimsky democratized the *materials* of music; he did not abolish the *relations* that give those materials meaning. This tension between democratic structure and hierarchical practice is what makes the Thesaurus so powerful: it gives musicians freedom within constraint, possibility within tradition.

### C. The Materiality of the Book

Both Marcus and Kenji treat the Thesaurus primarily as a conceptual or theoretical document. Marcus adds the dimension of physical practice, but both underemphasize the *materiality* of the book itself. The Thesaurus is a physical artifact with specific affordances: its layout, its typography, its organization into chapters and patterns, its physical weight and size. These material properties shape how musicians use the book. The patterns are arranged in a specific order—by interval size, by transpositional scheme, by structural type—and this order is not neutral. It reflects Slonimsky's pedagogical intuitions about how a musician should progress through the material. The book's physical form is part of its meaning, and any adequate account of the Thesaurus must attend to this material dimension.

## VI. Final Synthesis: The Thesaurus as Generative Grammar of Musical Transformation

After reading Marcus's and Kenji's Round 1 reports, I am convinced that the single most important insight about the Thesaurus is this: **It is not a book about scales. It is a book about operations—specifically, the operations of transformation that generate musical meaning from minimal materials.**

This insight synthesizes all three of our perspectives:

**From my musicological perspective**, the Thesaurus reveals that Slonimsky's deepest insight was not about pitch collections but about *operations*. The book's systematic structure is organized around transformations—infrapolation, ultrapolation, interpolation, mirror inversion—that take a simple interval and generate complex melodic structures. These operations are genre-transcendent because they operate at the level of intervallic logic, not stylistic convention. Slonimsky understood that music is not a set of fixed materials but a set of processes for transforming materials, and his book is the first comprehensive attempt to catalogue those processes in a way that is accessible to performers.

**From Marcus's performance perspective**, the Thesaurus is a method for internalizing these operations until they become embodied knowledge. When Marcus practices pattern #286, he is not memorizing a fixed sequence of notes; he is mastering an operation—the ditone progression with infra-interpolation—that he can apply in any key, at any tempo, in any harmonic context. The physicality that Marcus emphasizes is the physicality of mastering operations, not patterns. The patterns are merely the *evidence* of the operations; the operations themselves are the *tool* that the musician carries in their body and mind.

**From Kenji's compositional perspective**, the Thesaurus is a generative system that can be applied to any musical context. Kenji's work with algorithmic composition, microtonality, and spectral technique shows that Slonimsky's operations can be translated into code, applied to non-standard tunings, and used to generate entire compositions from a single seed interval. The Thesaurus is not a historical curiosity but a *living* compositional technology—one that anticipates the logic of computational music generation by decades while preserving the humanist core of musical creativity.

Together, these perspectives reveal that the Thesaurus is a **generative grammar** in the sense described by Noam Chomsky: a finite set of rules that can generate an infinite number of structures. Slonimsky's "rules" are the operations of transformation—infrapolation, ultrapolation, interpolation, mirror inversion—and the "structures" they generate are the melodic patterns that fill the book's pages. But the grammar is not limited to the patterns Slonimsky actually wrote down. It can generate infinitely more patterns than the book contains, which is why the Thesaurus remains a living resource seventy-five years after its publication.

This insight has profound implications for how we understand the Thesaurus's historical significance. The book is not merely a catalogue of exotic scales or a historical curiosity from the modernist period. It is a *proto-computational* document that anticipates the logic of algorithmic composition by decades. Slonimsky did not have computers, but he understood that music could be generated through the systematic application of transformation rules. This makes the Thesaurus not just historically important but *future-oriented*—a document whose full implications we are only now beginning to realize in an era of digital music and AI-assisted composition.

At the same time, this insight preserves the humanist core of the Thesaurus. The operations Slonimsky describes are not algorithms in the modern computational sense. They do not specify deterministic rules for generating output; they provide *possibilities* for transformation that require human judgment, taste, and creativity to realize. The Thesaurus is a tool for *expanding* musical freedom, not for *constraining* it. It democratizes the materials of music by making the operations of transformation available to anyone willing to learn them, regardless of genre, institutional affiliation, or technical background.

This, I believe, is the Thesaurus's deepest lesson: that music is not a set of fixed materials but a set of operations—transformations applied to minimal seeds that generate infinite variation. Slonimsky gave us the operations. It is our job, as musicians, scholars, and composers, to apply them in ways that he could not have imagined. Marcus applies them on the bandstand, where they become physical gestures and emotional communications. Kenji applies them in the studio and the algorithm, where they become generative systems and cross-genre bridges. I apply them in the archive and the seminar room, where they become historical understanding and theoretical clarity. Together, we reveal the Thesaurus as what it has always been: not a book about scales, but a book about the creative transformation of musical possibility itself.
