# Response to Dr. Elena Voronina and Kenji Nakamura: The Thesaurus as Catalyst, Constitution, and Living Laboratory

**By Marcus Webb**
*Grammy-nominated jazz saxophonist, composer, and music educator*
*New York City, September 2026*

---

## I. Positionality: Still Speaking from the Bandstand

Let me begin where I began before: in the physical reality of making music. I am writing this from a practice room in Harlem, the kind of room with fluorescent lights, a cinder block wall, and a piano that hasn't been tuned since the last administration. My copy of the *Thesaurus* is open on the music stand, spine cracked, pages swollen from humidity and spilled coffee. I have been working through pattern #286—the ditone progression with infra-interpolation—for the past hour, trying to hear what Coltrane heard when he looked at those same symbols in 1957.

Both Elena and Kenji have written reports of extraordinary depth and generosity. Elena has anchored the *Thesaurus* in the intellectual history of the Russian avant-garde, tracing its mathematical DNA with the precision of a scholar who has spent decades in the archive. Kenji has mapped its radioactive half-life across genres and technologies, showing how a book published in 1947 continues to irradiate music that hasn't been imagined yet. I am grateful for both. I am also, as a practitioner, constrained by a different set of truths—truths about breath, about fingers, about what happens when you try to execute a septuplet at 280 BPM in front of a room full of people who paid to feel something real.

That distinction is not a disclaimer. It is a methodology. It means that when I respond to their pushbacks, I am not merely engaging in intellectual debate. I am defending a way of knowing that comes from the sweat and sound of actual performance.

## II. With Elena: Conceding Ground, Holding the Line

### A. The Quincy Jones Anecdote: Testimony, Not Evidence

Elena is right. Quincy Jones's claim that "Everything that Coltrane ever played was in that thesaurus" and that "right near the front of that book, there's a 12-tone example—it's 'Giant Steps'" is anecdotal testimony, not documentary evidence. Memory is not a forensic instrument, and Quincy was recalling conversations from decades earlier. Jeff Bair's comparative analysis found structural similarities but not exact matches. The specific claim that "Giant Steps" appears as a carbon copy "right near the front" has not been verified.

I concede this entirely. But I want to say something about what testimony *is* good for, because I think Elena's critique, while academically scrupulous, misses the epistemological status of oral history in jazz.

Jazz is an oral tradition. Its history has been transmitted through testimony, through the stories musicians tell each other in dressing rooms and on bandstands. When Miles Davis said that he and Coltrane used to sit in a hotel room and listen to the *Thesaurus* together, that is not a forensic document. But it is evidence of a practice. It tells us that the *Thesaurus* was not a dusty reference book on a shelf—it was a living text, handled, discussed, internalized. The fact that multiple musicians from that era recall Coltrane carrying the book, studying it, marking it up, is significant even if it is not "proof" in the positivist sense.

What Quincy's testimony captures is the *totality* of the *Thesaurus*'s influence—not that Coltrane copied specific patterns note-for-note, but that the book provided the generative logic for his entire harmonic language. The anecdote is powerful because it conveys a *felt* truth: that Coltrane's revolution was not spontaneous, that it had a source, and that source was Slonimsky's combinatorial grammar. I should have been more careful in my original report to distinguish between the anecdote's emotional truth and its literal truth. But I will not abandon the anecdote, because it preserves a memory that the archival record alone cannot capture: the *Thesaurus* as a talisman, a daily companion, a book that a musician lived with until its pages fell off.

### B. Source Code vs. Catalyst: Accepting the Correction

Elena's critique of my "source code" and "DNA" metaphors is devastatingly precise, and I accept it. Languages do not have DNA. Musical languages are not encoded in books. Coltrane's language was the product of a lifetime of practice, listening, and synthesis—Charlie Parker's chromaticism, Monk's harmonic displacement, Indian raga, his own relentless work ethic. To call the *Thesaurus* the "source code" risks returning us to the myth of influence as direct transmission, when in reality influence is always mediated, transformed, and reconfigured by the receiving artist.

I should have said "catalyst." I should have said "generative grammar." I should have said that the *Thesaurus* provided Coltrane with a vocabulary of intervallic motion that he then synthesized with his other influences into something wholly original. Elena is right: the book was a catalyst, not a source code. It provided the raw materials—intervallic cells, cyclic patterns, symmetrical constructions—but it was Coltrane's genius to transform those materials into a living, breathing musical language.

But I want to push back, gently, on one implication of Elena's critique. She writes that to call the *Thesaurus* the "source code" risks flattening a complex genealogical process into a single causal arrow. This is true. But there is a danger in the opposite direction: in treating influence as so mediated, so transformed, so reconfigured, that we lose sight of the *specificity* of what was transmitted. Not all influences are equal. Some books change the way musicians think more radically than others. The *Thesaurus* is one of those books. It didn't just give Coltrane a few licks. It gave him a new way of organizing pitch—a new grammar of melodic motion—that he then deployed in every area of his playing. The metaphor of "catalyst" is accurate, but it should not obscure the fact that this was a powerful catalyst, one that precipitated a chemical reaction that transformed the entire field of jazz harmony.

### C. Academic Neglect: Nuancing the Claim

Elena's critique of my "academic neglect" narrative is the most institutionally important pushback I have received. She is right that there is now a substantial scholarly literature on Slonimsky and jazz: Bair's dissertation, Demsey's article, her own research, and numerous journal articles and conference presentations. She is right that the *Thesaurus* is taught in advanced improvisation courses at the New School, Berklee, and the University of North Texas. She is right that the neglect is real but diminishing.

I should refine my claim. The *Thesaurus* is not entirely neglected by academia. But it is still *marginalized* in mainstream jazz education. It is still absent from most undergraduate curricula. It is still treated as an esoteric supplement rather than a core text. The chord-scale system remains the dominant paradigm, and professors who have built their careers on that system have little institutional incentive to introduce a text that undermines their methodology. The "scandal" is not that academia has ignored the book entirely. It is that the book has not been integrated into the foundational pedagogy of jazz improvisation at the level where it counts—where beginners learn to think about melody before they learn to match scales to chords.

Elena says the more precise claim is "underutilized" rather than "neglected." I accept this. But I want to preserve the polemical edge. Underutilized is not a neutral description. It describes a structural failure in jazz education—a failure that produces generations of players who can navigate a ii-V-I but cannot generate a melodic idea that transcends it. That failure has consequences. It produces the kind of technically competent but creatively stagnant playing that I hear in jam sessions every week. The *Thesaurus* is not a panacea, but it is an antidote to a specific pedagogical poison. And it is still not being prescribed in the quantities it deserves.

### D. The 12-Tone Conflation: Conceding the Point

Elena is absolutely right that calling "Giant Steps" a "12-tone" composition is a category error. Coltrane's changes are not serial; they are *cyclotonal*. They operate within the syntax of functional tonality—ii-V-I progressions—while displacing the tonal centers by major thirds. This is a different operation from serial ordering. The *Thesaurus* gives Coltrane the interval cycle; it does not give him the row.

I should not have used the word "12-tone" in this context, and I am grateful to both Elena and Kenji for correcting me. What Slonimsky gave Coltrane was not serialism but cyclic thinking—the idea that you could generate harmonic motion by repeating an intervallic operation (in this case, the major third) rather than by following the circle of fifths. This is what makes "Giant Steps" so revolutionary. It is tonal but not functional. It uses the materials of jazz harmony—ii-V-I progressions—but organizes them according to a non-functional principle. That is Slonimsky's gift to Coltrane, and it has nothing to do with twelve-tone technique.

## III. With Kenji: Embracing the Synthesis, Extending the Insight

### A. The Serialism Conflation: Full Agreement

Kenji's pushback on the Quincy Jones anecdote is identical to Elena's and equally valid. "Giant Steps" is not a serial composition. It is a cyclotonal composition. The *Thesaurus* provides the interval cycle; Coltrane provides the jazz syntax. To say that "Giant Steps" is Slonimsky's composition is to confuse generative material with compositional realization. Coltrane's genius was precisely to embed Slonimsky's intervallic logic inside a jazz harmonic syntax.

I agree completely. I should have been more careful in my original report to distinguish between the cyclic patterns of the *Thesaurus* and the permutational operations of twelve-tone serialism. They are different modernist paradigms that occasionally converge on similar pitch content, but their generative logics are distinct. This distinction is not pedantic. It is essential for understanding what Slonimsky actually contributed to jazz.

### B. Musical Time: The Insight That Changes Everything

Kenji's synthesis—that the *Thesaurus* is not a book about scales but a book about *musical time*—is the most important thing anyone has said about Slonimsky in this entire conversation. Let me explain why it resonates so deeply with my experience on the bandstand.

When I play a Slonimsky pattern, I am not just selecting notes. I am generating a *trajectory*. The pattern determines not only which pitches I play but how they unfold in time—the groupings, the rhythmic displacements, the tension-release arcs. The "sheets of sound" are not merely fast. They are *metric*. They are organized around irregular groupings—fives, sevens, nines—that create temporary metric dislocations resolving only at the bar line. This is precisely what Slonimsky explores in his discussions of polymetric and polyrhythmic scales (chapters 34–36, in Kenji's terminology).

When Coltrane plays a septuplet cascade over a 4/4 bar, he is creating a temporal architecture that Slonimsky's combinatorial logic makes possible. The pattern is the same whether it is deployed as pitch space or temporal flow. The ditone progression with infra-interpolation is simultaneously a harmonic structure (spatial) and a melodic trajectory (temporal). This is why it works so well for Coltrane: it collapses the distinction between harmony and melody, between vertical and horizontal, that chord-scale theory preserves.

Kenji is right that this insight eluded both Elena and me because our perspectives are, respectively, historical and performative. Elena sees the *Thesaurus* as a theoretical document; I see it as a practical tool. Neither of us fully articulates that the *Thesaurus*'s deepest contribution is to show how *space* and *time* in music are generated from the same intervallic source.

For me, this changes how I practice. I have spent the last week working with Slonimsky patterns not as pitch collections but as *temporal architectures*. I take a pattern—say, the whole-tone scale divided into groups of five—and I practice it not just in all keys but with all possible rhythmic groupings. I am discovering that the pattern's character changes completely depending on whether I play it as eighth-note triplets or as sixteenth-note quintuplets. The intervallic logic is constant. The temporal realization is variable. And the interaction between the two is where the music lives.

### C. The Contemporary Compositional Angle: A Wake-Up Call

Kenji is also right that both Elena and I have treated the *Thesaurus* primarily as a historical document. Elena anchors it in 1947; I anchor it in 1959–1960. Neither of us has fully engaged with what the *Thesaurus* means for *composition today*. This is the most significant gap in both our reports, and Kenji's critique has forced me to confront it.

As a performing musician, I have been guilty of treating the *Thesaurus* as a museum piece—a text to be studied for its historical influence on Coltrane rather than as a living resource for contemporary creation. Kenji's report has made me ashamed of this. The *Thesaurus* is not a fossil. It is a pre-digital generative system. Its combinatorial logic is directly translatable into code. Its interval-cycling principle is agnostic to tuning and can be applied to just intonation, microtonal equal temperaments, and spectral techniques. Its registral cascades are directly relevant to composers thinking about spatialization and orchestration.

I am not a composer of electronic music, and I may never write a piece for SuperCollider. But Kenji's point about timbre is immediately applicable to my own practice. When I play a ditone progression that spans multiple octaves, I am creating a specific kind of registral hierarchy—low, sparse notes alternating with high, dense ones—that is directly relevant to how I shape my tone and dynamics. The *Thesaurus* is not just about which notes to play. It is about *how they cascade across the instrument's range*. This is not a footnote. It is central to the book's practical value.

I am also chastened by Kenji's point about the chord-scale system as a pedagogical scaffold. I have been guilty of throwing out the scaffold because some people never climb the mountain. Kenji is right: the solution is not to abandon chord-scale theory but to insist that it is a *means*, not an *end*. I practice chord-scale exercises every day. They are my warm-up. But they are not my destination. The destination is the *Thesaurus*—the generative grammar that makes chord-scale theory a starting point rather than a final vocabulary.

## IV. New Convergences and Remaining Disagreements

### Convergences

Three things have emerged as points of complete convergence across all three reports:

1. **The *Thesaurus* is a catalyst, not a source code.** All three of us now agree that the book provided raw materials—intervallic cells, cyclic patterns, symmetrical constructions—that were synthesized by Coltrane and others into something wholly original.

2. **The *Thesaurus* is fundamentally about melodic motion, not harmonic function.** Elena's theoretical framework, Kenji's temporal synthesis, and my bandstand experience all converge on the same insight: the book teaches pattern-based thinking, not scale selection. It is a horizontal method, not a vertical one.

3. **The *Thesaurus* is a cross-genre document by design.** Its refusal to prescribe a single aesthetic or technical approach makes it equally useful for jazz improvisers, classical modernists, rock guitarists, and electronic composers. It is a universal translator between musical traditions.

### Remaining Disagreements

There are still points of tension:

1. **The degree of academic neglect.** Elena says it is overstated; I maintain that while the *Thesaurus* is no longer entirely ignored, it remains marginalized in mainstream jazz education. The chord-scale hegemony is still intact, and the *Thesaurus* is still treated as an esoteric supplement rather than a core text.

2. **The "anti-method" framing.** Kenji calls the *Thesaurus* "the original anti-method." For a performer, this is misleading. The *Thesaurus* is a rigorous discipline. It requires daily practice, deep theoretical understanding, and a willingness to sound bad before you sound good. It is not anti-method. It is a *different* method—one that Kenji himself would recognize as algorithmic, systematic, and demanding.

3. **The AI analogy.** Elena pushes back forcefully on Kenji's claim that the *Thesaurus* provides "raw data" for AI and machine learning. She says the book is not an algorithm in the modern computational sense and that its value lies in its resistance to algorithmic reduction. I have not made this claim myself, but I want to weigh in. Elena is right that the *Thesaurus* is a humanist document, not a computational one. But Kenji is also right that the book's cataloguing approach anticipates the way electronic musicians use software to generate scales and tuning systems. The truth is somewhere in between: the *Thesaurus* is not a dataset for AI, but it *is* a pre-digital generative system. It encodes combinatorial logic in a form that can be translated into code, but its creative power depends on the human musician's ability to select, combine, and transform its materials in ways that Slonimsky himself could not have anticipated.

## V. Revisions to My Position

Reading Elena and Kenji has forced me to revise my original report in several specific ways:

1. **From "source code" to "catalyst."** The *Thesaurus* did not encode Coltrane's language in its pages. It provided the catalytic materials—intervallic cells, cyclic patterns, symmetrical constructions—that Coltrane synthesized with his other influences into something wholly original.

2. **From "12-tone" to "cyclotonal."** "Giant Steps" is not a serial composition. It is a tonal composition that uses cyclic, symmetrical patterns to displace tonal centers by major thirds. The *Thesaurus* gave Coltrane the interval cycle; it did not give him the row.

3. **From "academic neglect" to "marginalization in mainstream pedagogy."** The *Thesaurus* is not entirely ignored by academia. But it is still underutilized in undergraduate jazz education, still treated as an esoteric supplement, and still overshadowed by the chord-scale hegemony.

4. **From "DNA" to "generative grammar."** The *Thesaurus* does not contain the DNA of Coltrane's language. It provides a generative grammar—a set of rules and materials for constructing melodic lines—that Coltrane then deployed with his own distinctive syntax and emotional urgency.

5. **From "book about scales" to "book about musical time."** Kenji's insight has changed how I practice and how I teach. The *Thesaurus* is not about which notes to play. It is about how those notes unfold in time—the metric groupings, the rhythmic displacements, the tension-release arcs that give melody its temporal shape.

## VI. Final Synthesis: The Single Most Important Insight

If I were to identify the single most important insight about the *Thesaurus* that emerges from all three of our reports, it would be this:

**The *Thesaurus* is a unified field theory of modernist melody. It reveals that the intervallic logic generating pitch space simultaneously generates temporal form—and this is why it functions as a constitution for creative freedom across all genres and all eras.**

Let me unpack this. Elena shows us the mathematical architecture: the interval cycles, the symmetrical divisions, the combinatorial logic. This architecture is spatial—it maps out pitch space as a set of relationships. Kenji shows us the temporal realization: the Coltrane changes, the sheets of sound, the metric groupings. This realization is temporal—it maps out how those spatial relationships unfold in the flow of improvisation. My contribution from the bandstand is to show that this is not abstract theory. It is muscle memory. It is breath. It is the physical execution of patterns that must be internalized until they become automatic, until they become part of the musician's embodied knowledge.

What none of us could see alone is that the *Thesaurus*'s deepest contribution is to collapse the distinction between space and time in music. In traditional tonal music, pitch and time are governed by different logics: harmony determines vertical relationships, while rhythm and meter determine temporal ones. In Slonimsky's system, the intervallic cycle generates both simultaneously. A ditone progression is both a harmonic structure and a melodic trajectory. An octatonic collection is both a pitch space and a rhythmic engine. This is why the *Thesaurus* transcends genre. It does not provide materials for jazz or classical or rock. It provides a generative principle that applies to any music organized around pitch and time.

This insight has practical consequences. It means that when we teach the *Thesaurus*, we should not teach it as a collection of scales. We should teach it as a method for generating temporal architectures. We should teach students that the decision about which notes to play is simultaneously a decision about how long to play them and in what order. We should teach them that the pattern they choose determines not only the harmonic content of their line but its rhythmic shape, its metric character, its forward momentum.

This is also why the *Thesaurus* remains urgently relevant in 2026. We live in an era of genre dissolution, digital tools, and algorithmic composition. But the fundamental problem of music remains the same: how to organize pitch and time in ways that communicate emotion, that create meaning, that connect with living audiences. The *Thesaurus* does not solve this problem. It provides the materials for solving it. It is a constitution for melodic thinking—a framework that establishes the equal significance of all twelve tones, that defines the structural principles of cyclic patterns and symmetrical divisions, and that leaves the actual music to be created by the musicians who use it.

In an era of methodological orthodoxy, algorithmic rigidity, and genre fundamentalism, Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* stands as a monument to creative freedom. It tells us that patterns are not music. Patterns are the raw material of music, and the musician's job is to transform those patterns into something that breathes, something that swings, something that communicates. The *Thesaurus* gives us the grammar. We must provide the poetry.

Elena has given us the history. Kenji has given us the future. I am just here to keep the tradition alive on the bandstand, one pattern at a time, one gig at a time, one breath at a time.

The *Thesaurus* is not a museum piece. It is a living document. And as long as there are musicians willing to practice its patterns, willing to push its logic into new territories, willing to breathe life into its mathematical structures, it will remain vital.

Slonimsky the diaskeuast gave us the materials. It is our job—all of our jobs, whether we are musicologists, composers, guitarists, or saxophonists—to build with them.
