# Nicolas Slonimsky's Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns (1947): A Cross-Genre Manifesto for the 21st Century

**By Kenji Nakamura**

---

## I. The Diaskeuast's Apocalypse

When Nicolas Slonimsky published *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* in 1947 through Coleman-Ross, he was not merely compiling a reference book. He was releasing a tactical weapon. A Russian-born polymath who had conducted the world premieres of Charles Ives's *Three Places in New England* and Edgard Varèse's *Ionisation*, Slonimsky understood that the 20th century's harmonic frontier had been violently displaced. The old major-minor system was not just dead—it was archaeological. What he constructed in the *Thesaurus* was nothing less than a systematic demolition of diatonic complacency, organized as 1,086 pages of pitch collections that make the traditional scale handbook look like a children's primer.

Slonimsky described himself as a *diaskeuast*—a Greek term meaning "reviser or interpolator." This self-designation is crucial. He was not a serialist, not a Webern disciple, not a Boulezian architect of total organization. He was something far more dangerous to the musical establishment: a cataloguer of chaos who believed that every possible melodic pattern, no matter how seemingly irrational, deserved to be notated, examined, and made available to composers and performers who lacked the theoretical arrogance to dismiss entire realms of pitch relationships. The *Thesaurus* is democratic. It does not judge. It simply presents.

The book's initial reception was, by Slonimsky's own admission, one of near-universal neglect. "It was largely ignored for years after its publication," he later noted. Yet it remained in print for over six decades. Its first champions were not academics but practicing musicians who discovered in its pages a kind of liberated pitch mathematics—a way to think about melody that bypassed the stultifying chord-scale theories of the conservatory. Among those who found the *Thesaurus* indispensable were some of the most iconoclastic improvisers and composers of the century: John Coltrane, Allan Holdsworth, Frank Zappa, Paul Grabowsky, and Steve Rochinski.

Quincy Jones captured the book's practical utility with characteristic concision in a 2018 interview: "Every time I used to see Coltrane, he'd have Nicolas Slonimsky's book." This is not an anecdote about a jazz curiosity. It is evidence that the *Thesaurus* functioned as a kind of underground hypertext for musicians who had exhausted the possibilities of chord-scale improvisation and needed a larger melodic cosmos. Coltrane's "sheets of sound"—those cascading, rhythmically compressed melodic avalanches—were not mere displays of technical prowess. They were the product of a mind systematically exploring intervallic cells, pattern permutations, and transpositional cycles drawn directly from Slonimsky's catalog. The *Thesaurus* gave Coltrane a vocabulary of melodic transformation that made his famous "Giant Steps" cycle seem almost conservative.

## II. Frank Zappa: The Avant-Garde Rock Immunized Against Genre

If any figure embodies the *Thesaurus*'s cross-genre destiny, it is Frank Zappa. Their relationship was not merely one of influence but of genuine personal friendship. In 1981, Slonimsky performed his own compositions at a Zappa concert in Santa Monica, California—a symbolic passing of the torch from the 20th century's greatest musical lexicographer to rock's most erudite iconoclast. Slonimsky, displaying his lifelong wit, later named his cat "Grody-to-the-Max" after learning the phrase from Zappa's daughter Moon.

Zappa's compositional language was, from its inception, a rejection of genre boundaries. His 1966 debut *Freak Out!* mixed satirical rock with extended sound collages, and this eclectic methodology never abandoned him. The *Thesaurus* provided Zappa with a systematic approach to melodic invention that transcended the pentatonic and blues-based vocabularies of rock guitar. Zappa's solos—particularly on albums like *Hot Rats* (1969), *The Grand Wazoo* (1972), and *Sheik Yerbouti* (1979)—exhibit a linear logic that can only be described as Slonimskian. They are not "rock" solos. They are not "jazz" solos. They are melodic constructions built from intervallic patterns that rotate, invert, and transpose with the rigor of a serialist composition but with the visceral impact of a guitar turned up to eleven.

Zappa's use of the octatonic scale, documented in works ranging from his early orchestral pieces to his late Synclavier compositions, is a direct line to Slonimsky. The octatonic collection—alternating whole and half steps, symmetrical, transpositionally invariant under multiples of three semitones—appears throughout Zappa's output as a means of generating harmonic ambiguity. In the title track of *The Black Page* (1980), Zappa constructs a melodic maze that cycles through octatonic subsets, creating the same "blurred, indistinct effect" that Walter Piston described in the whole-tone scale but with a more aggressive, staccato rhythmic profile. The *Thesaurus* gave Zappa permission to treat rock guitar as a vehicle for what he called "air sculptures"—melodic forms that exist purely in the domain of pitch and rhythm, unburdened by functional harmony.

Zappa's later electronic works, particularly those realized on the Synclavier, take the *Thesaurus* principle to its logical extreme. By eliminating the performative body entirely, Zappa's computer-generated compositions could realize melodic patterns of impossible speed and complexity. The *Thesaurus* had always been a kind of manual for algorithmic thinking about melody—each page a set of rules for generating musical lines. In Zappa's Synclavier period (1984–1993), these rules were implemented literally, with the computer executing transformations that would have been physically impossible for a human performer.

## III. Guitar as Intervallic Weapon: Holdsworth, Lane, and Buckethead

### Allan Holdsworth: The Saxophonist's Guitar

Allan Holdsworth (1946–2017) occupies a unique position in the history of the guitar. Trained initially on violin and drawn to the horn-like legato phrasing of saxophonists John Coltrane and Michael Brecker, Holdsworth approached the guitar as an instrument capable of producing "air sculptures" remarkably similar to Zappa's conception. His Wikipedia entry documents his "esoteric and idiosyncratic usage of advanced music theory concepts, especially with respect to melody and harmony," noting his reliance on "scale forms often derived from those such as the Lydian, diminished, harmonic major, augmented, whole tone, chromatic and altered scales."

The connection to Slonimsky is not merely stylistic but methodological. Holdsworth's approach to the fretboard—visualizing chord-scale relationships as symmetrical patterns rather than positional shapes—is a direct application of the *Thesaurus*'s intervallic logic. The whole-tone scale, which appears repeatedly in Holdsworth's work, is Forte set class 6-35, a maximally even, degenerate well-formed collection. In Slonimsky's hands, this scale is presented as one of many possible melodic organizations; in Holdsworth's, it becomes a means of generating that characteristic "outside" sound—tonally ambiguous, harmonically unresolved, melodically fluid.

Holdsworth's influence on subsequent generations of guitarists cannot be overstated. Eddie Van Halen declared him "the best, in my book." Frank Zappa lauded him as "one of the most interesting guys on guitar on the planet." Robben Ford called him "the John Coltrane of the guitar." But Holdsworth's true legacy is the conceptual framework he passed to guitarists who came of age in the 1980s and 1990s: Shawn Lane, Brett Garsed, Frank Gambale, and others who saw in his playing a dissolution of the boundary between jazz vocabulary and rock technique.

### Shawn Lane: The Piano-to-Guitar Transposition

Shawn Lane (1963–2003) represents the next generational transmission of the Slonimsky-Holdsworth lineage. Born in Memphis, Lane began on piano before switching to guitar at age ten. At fifteen, he saw Allan Holdsworth perform with U.K. and immediately recognized a path forward. Lane's playing—documented on albums like *Powers of Ten* (1992) and *The Tri-Tone Fascination* (1999)—is characterized by a horizontal intervallic approach that mirrors Holdsworth's but pushes it into even more extreme tempos. Where Holdsworth emphasized legato and saxophone-like phrasing, Lane added a precision-picked articulation that made his lines sound like computer-generated sequences executed by a human hand.

Lane's collaborations with bassist Jonas Hellborg, particularly the albums *Abstract Logic* (1995) and *Temporal Analogues of Paradise* (1996), represent some of the most advanced cross-genre fusion of the 1990s. The trio's music—Hellborg's electronically processed acoustic bass, Lane's guitar and keyboards, drummer Jeff Sipe's polymetric percussion—creates a sound world that is simultaneously jazz, rock, and electronic music. The melodic material frequently draws on octatonic and whole-tone collections, generating the same tonal ambiguity that characterized Coltrane's "sheets of sound" but within a harmonic framework that owes as much to Indian classical music as to Western theory.

Lane's legacy, cut short by his death at forty, is preserved in the work of guitarists who recognized his revolutionary approach. Buckethead, Paul Gilbert, and Rusty Cooley all cite Lane as a primary influence. Guitar World magazine noted in 2008 that "Few, if any, guitarists can play faster than Lane could, and his arpeggio sweeps and precision-picked lines blasted more rapid-fire notes than the average human mind could comprehend." This technical virtuosity was not ends in itself but the necessary precondition for executing melodic patterns of extreme intervactic density—the very patterns Slonimsky catalogued in the *Thesaurus*.

### Buckethead: The Theatrical Dimension

Brian Patrick Carroll (born 1969), known professionally as Buckethead, completes this lineage with a performer who transformed the guitar into a character—an anonymous, masked figure whose musical output spans avant-garde metal, funk, bluegrass, and ambient music. Buckethead's discography, currently numbering over eight hundred "Pike" releases as of 2026, is a living archive of cross-genre exploration. His 1992 debut *Bucketheadland*, released on John Zorn's Japanese Avant label, announced a guitarist who had absorbed the lessons of Zappa's genre-aversion, Holdsworth's harmonic adventurousness, and Slonimsky's intervallic democracy.

Buckethead's use of the octatonic scale is well-documented. Guitar World credited him with "ushering in a new era of virtuosity" while ranking *Bucketheadland* the 45th greatest moment in electric guitar history. His music for the *Guitar Hero II* video game (2006), particularly the track "Jordan," features a solo constructed from octatonic and whole-tone patterns that would not be out of place in a Ligeti etude. The piece's structured improvisation—a solo created specifically for the game—represents a fusion of Slonimskian pattern generation with the interactive possibilities of digital media.

## IV. Spectral Music: The Acoustic Turn

The connection between Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* and spectral music may not be immediately obvious, but it is profound. Spectral music—a movement that originated in France in the 1970s with composers Gérard Grisey, Tristan Murail, and Michaël Lévinas—takes the acoustic properties of sound as its primary compositional material. As Joshua Fineberg puts it, spectral music represents "a recognition that music is ultimately sound evolving in time."

Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* is, in essence, a pre-digital spectral analysis tool. By cataloguing every possible scale and melodic pattern, Slonimsky created a reference work that treated pitch collections as objects of study independent of tonal function. This is precisely the spectralist attitude: not "how does this chord function within a key?" but "what are the acoustic and perceptual properties of this specific arrangement of frequencies?" The *Thesaurus* does not provide functional analyses. It provides raw material.

The connection to equal divisions of the octave (EDO) and microtonality is equally direct. Slonimsky's catalog includes scales derived from non-Western tuning systems, equal temperaments beyond the standard twelve-tone system, and just intonation collections. Spectral composers working at IRCAM in Paris developed computer-based tools for analyzing and synthesizing sound spectra, but Slonimsky had already performed a similar labor by hand. The *Thesaurus* can be read as a proto-spectral document—a massive, encyclopedic exploration of the pitch universe that anticipates by decades the computer-assisted analyses of the spectralists.

Olivier Messiaen, whose seven "modes of limited transposition" form a direct conceptual bridge between Slonimsky's catalog and spectral thinking, called the whole-tone scale his "first mode" and the octatonic scale his "second mode." Messiaen's modes—symmetrical, transpositionally limited, acoustically distinctive—are essentially curated selections from the *Thesaurus*. A composer like Grisey, whose *Espaces Acoustiques* (1975) uses the harmonic series as its primary structural principle, would have found in Slonimsky's pages a ready-made vocabulary of non-functional pitch collections. The *Thesaurus* does not prescribe how to use its materials; it simply makes them available. This open-endedness is its genius.

## V. Electronic Music and Computer-Generated Composition: The Thesaurus as Algorithm

In the domain of electronic music and computer-generated composition, Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* functions as a kind of algorithmic cookbook. Each scale and pattern in the book is, in effect, a rule set for generating musical lines. The computer does not care about voice leading or tonal function; it cares about rules. The *Thesaurus* provides rules.

Composers like Iannis Xenakis, who used stochastic methods and mathematical models to generate musical structures, would have found in the *Thesaurus* a valuable companion text. Xenakis's *Metastasis* (1953–54), with its glissandi and pitch clouds generated from statistical distributions, represents an extreme of computer-assisted composition, but the underlying pitch collections could just as easily have been drawn from Slonimsky's catalog. The *Thesaurus* offers a middle path: not the total determinism of serialism, not the total randomness of stochasticism, but a curated selection of pitch relations that preserve melodic coherence while abandoning tonal hierarchy.

In contemporary electronic music—from the algorithmic compositions of Brian Eno to the microtonal explorations of electronic music producer Ellen Fullman—the *Thesaurus* remains relevant. Its cataloguing approach anticipates the way electronic musicians today use software to generate scales, tuning systems, and melodic patterns that would have been inconceivable in 1947. The book's lack of prescriptive aesthetic makes it compatible with any technology: it can be realized on a piano, a guitar, a computer, or a theremin.

## VI. Specific Scale Types: The Architecture of Dissonance

### The Prometheus Scale

Alexander Scriabin's "Prometheus chord"—a whole-tone collection with one note raised a semitone—represents one of the most famous extensions of the whole-tone scale. In Slonimsky's catalog, this collection appears as a distinct scale type, its acoustic properties precisely notated. The Prometheus scale (C, D, E, F#, G#, A#, B, with added D# in some versions) creates a harmonic field that is simultaneously whole-tone and bitonal. It was used by Scriabin in his *Prometheus: Poem of Fire* (1910) and later by composers seeking a "mystic" harmonic language. The scale's lack of perfect fourths and fifths above the tonic makes it acoustically unstable—a quality that Slonimsky's systematic presentation normalizes, making it available as a tool rather than a mystery.

### The Whole-Tone Scale

The whole-tone scale (Forte 6-35) is perhaps the most famous symmetrical collection in Western music. Its six pitches, all separated by whole steps, create a "blurred, indistinct effect" in which no single tone stands out. Slonimsky's treatment of the whole-tone scale is exhaustive: he presents all transpositions, all melodic patterns, all possible rhythmic subdivisions. For guitarists like Holdsworth, the whole-tone scale provides a vocabulary for "outside" playing—lines that float above the changes without resolving to chord tones. For Coltrane, it provided a means of generating "sheets of sound" that obscured tonal boundaries. For Zappa, it was a tool for creating harmonic ambiguity in rock contexts.

### The Octatonic Scale

The octatonic scale (Forte 8-28)—alternating whole and half steps, three distinct transpositions, symmetrical under inversion—is the most harmonically versatile of Slonimsky's catalogued collections. Its subsets include diminished seventh chords, augmented triads, and all six interval classes, making it a kind of harmonic Swiss Army knife. Stravinsky's *Petrushka* (1911) made the octatonic collection famous, but Slonimsky's systematic presentation reveals its full melodic potential. The scale's use in jazz—by Coltrane, by Miles Davis, by Wayne Shorter—transforms it from a harmonic color into a melodic engine. Its three transpositions (C#, D, Eb) create a cyclic pattern of modulation that avoids the dominant-tonic resolution of tonal harmony. In the hands of Holdsworth, the octatonic scale generates lines that seem to modulate continuously without ever settling. In the hands of Dream Theater or Opeth, it generates the dissonant, tonally ambiguous sound that defines progressive metal.

### The Enigmatic Scale

The enigmatic scale—invented by Adolfo Crescentini in 1888 and used by Verdi in his *Ave Maria*—represents the kind of exotic collection that Slonimsky treats with equal seriousness. Its intervallic structure (semitone, tone-and-a-half, tone, tone, tone, semitone, semitone) creates a harmonic field that is neither major nor minor, neither whole-tone nor octatonic. Guitarist Joe Satriani used it in "The Enigmatic" (1986), and it appears in the Yellowjackets' "Enigma" (1989). For Slonimsky, the enigmatic scale is proof of his democratic method: he includes collections that have no functional harmonic purpose but possess melodic interest. This is the *Thesaurus*'s core contribution—the expansion of the melodic imagination beyond the limits of functional tonality.

## VII. "Beyond Slonimsky": Rich Cochrane and the Digital Frontier

Rich Cochrane's work on extending Slonimsky's methods represents the most significant theoretical development since the *Thesaurus* itself. Cochrane, a mathematician and musician based in the UK, has developed software tools and theoretical frameworks that generate scales and melodic patterns beyond Slonimsky's original catalog. His approach treats the *Thesaurus* not as a finished product but as a starting point—a proof of concept for what systematic scale generation can achieve.

Cochrane's work intersects with set theory and transformational theory in important ways. By applying the mathematical tools of group theory to pitch collections, Cochrane generates new scales with specified symmetry properties, intervallic structures, and transformational behaviors. This represents a shift from Slonimsky's encyclopedic method—cataloguing every possible scale—to a generative method—creating scales with desired properties. The implications for cross-genre musicians are profound: it is now possible to design custom scales for specific musical contexts, whether that context is a jazz solo, an electronic composition, or a progressive metal riff.

Cochrane's "Beyond Slonimsky" project also addresses the equal divisions of the octave (EDO) that Slonimsky touched upon but could not fully explore due to the limitations of 12-tone notation. In 17-EDO, 22-EDO, 31-EDO, and beyond, intervallic relationships that are dissonant or impossible in 12-EDO become available. Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* can be seen as a 12-EDO document, but its methodology applies to any EDO. Cochrane's extensions make this explicit, providing tools for composers and performers working in microtonal systems.

## VIII. Set Theory and Transformational Theory: The Mathematical Subtext

The *Thesaurus* predates the formal development of pitch-class set theory by Allen Forte by nearly two decades, but its methodology is set-theoretic in practice. Every scale in the *Thesaurus* can be translated into Forte set-class notation, subjected to transposition and inversion operations, and analyzed for symmetry properties. The whole-tone scale is 6-35, the octatonic scale is 8-28, the enigmatic scale is 7-31 (with specific interval content). Slonimsky's presentation of these collections as melodic patterns rather than harmonic objects anticipates the set-theoretical approach by showing that the same pitch collection can generate infinite melodic variations depending on the order and rhythm of its constituent pitches.

Transformational theory, developed by David Lewin in the 1980s, takes this a step further by focusing on the relationships between pitch collections rather than the collections themselves. A Lewin transformation—an operation that maps one pitch or collection to another—is precisely what Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* provides in practice. When Slonimsky presents a pattern transposed to all twelve keys, he is demonstrating a transpositional transformation (Tn). When he presents inversions of melodic cells, he is demonstrating an inversional transformation (In). The *Thesaurus* is a transformational document avant la lettre.

For the 21st-century cross-genre musician, this mathematical subtext is essential. It means that the *Thesaurus* is not a book of scales to be memorized but a manual of transformations to be applied. A guitarist can take a whole-tone pattern from Slonimsky and apply it over a dominant chord as a tritone substitute. A composer can take an octatonic collection and use it to generate a melodic sequence that modulates by minor thirds. An electronic musician can use a Slonimsky pattern as the basis for an algorithmic composition that generates infinite variations.

## IX. Why the Thesaurus Is Essential for 21st-Century Cross-Genre Musicians

The argument for Slonimsky's *Thesaurus* in the 21st century is not nostalgic. It is tactical. Here is why every serious cross-genre musician—guitarist, composer, electronic producer, improviser—needs this book:

**1. It is the original anti-method.** In an era of method books, apps, and online courses that prescribe specific scales for specific chords, the *Thesaurus* offers something radical: a comprehensive catalog of pitch collections with no prescriptive aesthetic attached. It does not tell you how to play over a ii-V-I. It shows you what happens when you play a whole-tone scale over a dominant chord, when you superimpose an octatonic collection on a major seventh harmony, when you sequence an enigmatic pattern in minor thirds. The music you make with these materials is up to you.

**2. It is the common language of avant-garde traditions.** John Coltrane's sheets of sound, Frank Zappa's rock operas, Allan Holdsworth's legato lines, Olivier Messiaen's modes of limited transposition, and the spectral music of Grisey and Murail all share a common methodological root: the systematic exploration of non-diatonic pitch collections. The *Thesaurus* is the Rosetta Stone that translates between these traditions. A jazz guitarist who learns the octatonic scale from the *Thesaurus* is using the same collection that Stravinsky used in *Petrushka*, that Bartók used in his *Fourth Quartet*, and that Dream Theater uses in "Octavarium."

**3. It is a tool for algorithmic thinking.** In an age when electronic musicians use Max/MSP, SuperCollider, and Ableton Live to generate musical material, the *Thesaurus* provides the raw data—the pitch collections, intervallic patterns, and transformational rules—that can be fed into these systems. Each page of the *Thesaurus* is a potential algorithm. The book's encyclopedic scope makes it a valuable resource for anyone designing software that generates melodies, harmonies, or entire compositions.

**4. It is a repository of microtonal and non-Western knowledge.** Slonimsky's catalog includes scales from non-Western traditions—Indian ragas, Indonesian gamelan tunings, Persian modal systems—that Western musicians might otherwise never encounter. In a globalized musical landscape, this knowledge is essential. The *Thesaurus* provides a notational framework for understanding and incorporating these tunings into Western instruments and electronic systems.

**5. It is a challenge to tonal fundamentalism.** The *Thesaurus* is, at its core, a political document. It challenges the hegemony of the major-minor system by demonstrating that the pitch universe is far larger—and far more interesting—than seven notes repeated in octaves. For musicians working in genres that privilege tonal harmony—pop, rock, folk, much jazz—the *Thesaurus* offers an escape route. It provides a vocabulary for writing or improvising music that sounds "outside" without being random or chaotic.

**6. It connects to the cutting edge of music technology.** As artificial intelligence and machine learning begin to compose music, the *Thesaurus* provides a training dataset of unprecedented scope. An AI trained on Slonimsky's patterns could generate melodies in any style, from Baroque to bebop to black metal, by learning the transformational relationships between pitch collections. The book's systematic organization makes it ideal for computational analysis.

## X. Conclusion: The Infinite Melodic Universe

Nicolas Slonimsky lived to be 101, dying in Los Angeles in 1995. He saw his *Thesaurus* go from neglected curiosity to essential reference, used by guitarists in rock bands, saxophonists in jazz quartets, and composers in electronic studios. He saw his friend Frank Zappa transform the possibilities of rock music by applying avant-garde classical techniques to the electric guitar. He saw Allan Holdsworth create a new language for the instrument by drawing on the same symmetrical collections that Messiaen and Grisey used in the concert hall.

The *Thesaurus of Scales and Melodic Patterns* is not a book you read once and put away. It is a book you return to throughout your musical life, finding new patterns, new transformations, new possibilities each time. It is a map of the infinite melodic universe, and like any good map, it does not tell you where to go—it shows you where you *can* go.

For the 21st-century cross-genre musician, operating in a world where genre boundaries have dissolved, where electronic tools make any tuning system available, where collaboration across musical traditions is routine, the *Thesaurus* is more relevant than ever. It is the original cross-genre document: equally useful to a jazz saxophonist, a rock guitarist, a classical composer, an electronic producer, and a hip-hop producer sampling from the infinite library of pitch relationships.

Slonimsky the diaskeuast gave us the materials. It is our job to build with them.
